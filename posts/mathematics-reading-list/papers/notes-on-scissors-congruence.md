## Before you start

These notes run to 35 pages and move fast from cutting up triangles to group homology, Bloch groups and motivic cohomology. You may feel a drop in the stomach around page 7, when the homological algebra starts. That feeling is fair. The notes are lecture notes, and some proofs are left unfinished. If you want to see why a cube and a regular tetrahedron of equal volume are different, the first three pages already give it. Enjoy those. The rest can wait.

## The story

In the plane, two polygons of equal area can always be cut into finitely many pieces and reassembled into each other. People wanted the same in three dimensions. Dehn blocked it. He attached to a polyhedron the sum, over its edges, of length ⊗ dihedral angle, and showed that cutting never changes this sum. The cube gives zero. The regular tetrahedron does not, because its dihedral angle arccos(1/3) is not a rational multiple of π. So volume alone fails. The question then becomes whether volume and the Dehn invariant together are enough. Sydler proved that they are, and Jessen found exactly which values the invariant takes. The notes then repeat the game in hyperbolic space, where volume becomes a dilogarithm and the scissors group turns into the Bloch group.

## Main definitions

**Scissors congruence group P(E<sup>n</sup>).** Two polyhedra in Euclidean space E<sup>n</sup> are equidecomposable if each can be cut along finitely many planes and the pieces moved by orientation-preserving isometries to form the other. Classes form a semigroup under disjoint union, and P(E<sup>n</sup>) is its Grothendieck group.
- Prisms: the product of a polygon with an interval. They span a copy of P(E<sup>2</sup>) inside P(E<sup>3</sup>).
- Modulo prisms: the quotient P<sub>1</sub>(E<sup>3</sup>) = P(E<sup>3</sup>)/P(E<sup>2</sup>) is a real vector space under dilation.

**Dehn invariant.** For a polyhedron P in E<sup>3</sup>, D(P) = Σ ℓ(e) ⊗ α(e) in ℝ ⊗<sub>ℤ</sub> ℝ/πℤ. The sum runs over the edges e, with ℓ the length and α the dihedral angle.

**Kähler differentials and the map J.** Ω<sup>1</sup><sub>ℝ/ℤ</sub> is the real vector space spanned by symbols dr for r ∈ ℝ, with d additive, d(rs) = r ds + s dr, and dr = 0 for rational r. The map J sends ℓ ⊗ α to ℓ · d(sin α)/cos α when α is not a rational multiple of π.

**Bloch group B<sub>2</sub>(F).** For a field F, the abelian group generated by symbols [a], a ∈ F, modulo the five-term relation. The notes obtain it by dropping the last defining relation of the ideal hyperbolic scissors group P(∂H<sup>3</sup>).

## Main results

**Theorem (Bolyai–Gerwien; Theorem 1.1).** Two finite Euclidean planar polygons are equidecomposable if and only if they have the same area.
- Step 1: turn any triangle into a rectangle with one side of length 1, through a parallelogram and shears.
- Step 2: cut a polygon into triangles and stack the resulting rectangles.

**Theorem (Zylev; Theorem 1.3).** In every dimension n, if P + R is equidecomposable with Q + R, then P is equidecomposable with Q. So the semigroup of classes injects into P(E<sup>n</sup>).

**Theorem (Dehn–Sydler–Jessen; Theorem 1.7).** The sequence 0 → P(E<sup>2</sup>) → P(E<sup>3</sup>) → ℝ ⊗<sub>ℤ</sub> ℝ/πℤ → Ω<sup>1</sup><sub>ℝ/ℤ</sub> → 0 is exact, with maps the prism map, D and J. In words: volume and Dehn invariant together classify polyhedra in E<sup>3</sup> up to scissors congruence, and J cuts out the image of D.
- Step 1: reduce to orthoschemes, where D is a 2-coboundary, and prove Sydler's Lemma (Theorem 1.10), a four-term identity between orthoschemes modulo prisms.
- Step 2: compare with an auxiliary complex and the Cathelineau complex to build a left inverse to D.

**Theorem (Dupont; Theorem 2.3).** For n ≥ 2 the map from finite hyperbolic polyhedra to semi-ideal ones, P(H<sup>n</sup>) → P(H̄<sup>n</sup>), is an isomorphism. For n = 3 the map from ideal polyhedra P(∂H<sup>3</sup>) is an isomorphism too. For other odd n > 2 it is an isomorphism modulo 2-torsion.

## Note, then ignore

- Unfinished proofs. The notes say outright that the step "kernel of J equals image of D" is still to be written, and the Cathelineau section is a sketch. The theorem is standard. Safe to skip on a first reading.
- The tensor is over ℤ. That is why a rational multiple of π contributes nothing to D, and why the tetrahedron argument works. Note it once, then skip.
- The formula for J. It looks odd because J "wants to be" ℓ ⊗ α ↦ ℓ dα, and the sine form makes that algebraic. Safe to skip.
- Negative pieces. In the proof of Sydler's Lemma some pieces overlap and count with negative sign. This is bookkeeping in the group. Safe to skip.
- Spherical and hyperbolic Zylev. The notes remark that the result still holds there but is harder to prove. Safe to skip.
