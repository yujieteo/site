slogan: The value L(E,2) of a modular elliptic curve is π times an elliptic dilogarithm of a divisor, and explicit algebraic conditions say which divisors work.
basis: the introduction and the main theorem statements

## Before you start

This paper asks for K-theory, regulators, heights and biextensions, often in one sentence. The likely feeling is shame at how much background seems to be assumed. That feeling is allowed, and it says nothing about whether you may read on. The heart of the paper is a formula you can test on a computer: a special value of an L-function equals a sum of a dilogarithm-like function at points of a curve. If that kind of formula is what you came for, start with the numerical example and enjoy it.

## The story

Zagier conjectured that ζ<sub>F</sub>(2) for a number field F is a combination of values of the Bloch-Wigner dilogarithm at elements of F, and that an algebraic condition picks the right combinations. The condition comes from the Bloch-Suslin complex, which computes the relevant K-group. For an elliptic curve E over ℚ, Bloch had averaged the dilogarithm over the lattice to get an elliptic dilogarithm on E(ℂ), and Beilinson had tied L(E, 2) to a regulator on K<sub>2</sub>(E). What was missing was the algebraic condition: which divisors on E give L(E, 2)? Zagier guessed two conditions from computer experiments with Cohen. Goncharov and Levin build an elliptic version of the Bloch-Suslin complex, show that it computes the weight two part of K<sub>2</sub>(E), and so prove that Zagier's conditions are the right ones.

## Main definitions

**Elliptic dilogarithm.** The Bloch-Wigner function is L<sub>2</sub>(z) = Im Li<sub>2</sub>(z) + arg(1 − z) · log|z|. Write E(ℂ) = ℂ<sup>\*</sup>/q<sup>ℤ</sup>. The elliptic dilogarithm is L<sub>2,q</sub>(z) = Σ<sub>n ∈ ℤ</sub> L<sub>2</sub>(q<sup>n</sup>z). The sum converges, and L<sub>2,q</sub> extends by linearity to divisors P = Σ n<sub>j</sub>(P<sub>j</sub>) on E(ℂ).

**The group B<sub>2</sub>(E).** For an elliptic curve E over a field k with Jacobian J, an extension 0 → k<sup>\*</sup> → B<sub>2</sub>(E) → S<sup>2</sup>J(k) → 0 built from Deligne's pairing of degree zero line bundles. A point a of E gives an element {a}<sub>2</sub> that maps to a · a in S<sup>2</sup>J. It plays the role that a theta function would play.
- Over a local field K: there is a homomorphism H : B<sub>2</sub>(E/K) → ℝ that restricts to log|x| on K<sup>\*</sup>, and H({a}<sub>2</sub>) is the canonical local height of a.

**Elliptic motivic complex B(E; 3).** For k algebraically closed, B<sub>3</sub>(E) = ℤ[E(k)]/R<sub>3</sub>(E), where R<sub>3</sub>(E) is a subgroup of relations that the elliptic dilogarithm satisfies when k = ℂ. The complex is B<sub>3</sub>(E) → B<sub>2</sub>(E) ⊗ J → J ⊗ Λ<sup>2</sup>J → Λ<sup>3</sup>J, and the first map sends {a} to −(1/2){a}<sub>2</sub> ⊗ a.
- Over a general field: take Galois invariants of the complex over the algebraic closure.

## Main results

**Theorem (L(E, 2) for modular curves; Theorem 1.1).** Let E be a modular elliptic curve over ℚ. Then there is a ℚ-rational divisor P = Σ n<sub>j</sub>(P<sub>j</sub>) with points over the algebraic closure of ℚ such that L(E, 2) = c · π · L<sub>2,q</sub>(P) for some non-zero rational c, and P satisfies: (a) Σ n<sub>j</sub> P<sub>j</sub> ⊗ P<sub>j</sub> ⊗ P<sub>j</sub> = 0 in S<sup>3</sup>J, (b) Σ n<sub>j</sub> h<sub>v</sub>(P<sub>j</sub>) · P<sub>j</sub> = 0 in J ⊗ ℝ for every valuation v of the field generated by the points, where h<sub>v</sub> is the canonical local height, (c) an integrality condition at each prime of split multiplicative reduction.
- Step 1: Bloch's regulator computation and Beilinson's theorem for modular curves give L(E, 2) as a regulator of an element of K<sub>2</sub>(E).
- Step 2: the elliptic motivic complex shows that every such regulator value is L<sub>2,q</sub> of a divisor that satisfies (a) and (b).

**Theorem (regulators and divisors; Theorem 1.2).** Let E be any elliptic curve over ℚ. For each γ in K<sub>2</sub>(E) there is a ℚ-rational divisor P satisfying (a) and (b) whose elliptic dilogarithm equals the regulator of γ up to a non-zero rational factor. Conversely, each such P comes from some γ in K<sub>2</sub>(E) ⊗ ℚ.

**Theorem (the complex computes K-theory; Theorem 1.5).** For k algebraically closed, a sequence links H<sup>0</sup>(E, K<sub>2</sub>)/K<sub>2</sub>(k), a group B<sub>3</sub><sup>\*</sup>(E) that is a variant of B<sub>3</sub>(E), the group k<sup>\*</sup> ⊗ J, and the kernel of H<sup>1</sup>(E, K<sub>2</sub>) → k<sup>\*</sup>. It is exact at k<sup>\*</sup> ⊗ J and exact up to 2-torsion elsewhere.

**Corollary (conditional converse; Corollary 1.3).** If the image of the integral part of K<sub>2</sub>(E) under the regulator is L(E, 2) · ℚ, as Bloch and Beilinson predict, then every ℚ-rational divisor satisfying (a), (b), (c) gives q · L(E, 2) = π · L<sub>2,q</sub>(P) with q rational, possibly 0.

## Note, then ignore

- One condition, not two. Conditions (a) and (b) together say Σ n<sub>j</sub> {P<sub>j</sub>}<sub>2</sub> ⊗ P<sub>j</sub> = 0 in B<sub>2</sub>(E) ⊗ J (Lemma 1.4). Safe to skip on a first reading.
- Sixth root of unity. The map a ↦ {a}<sub>2</sub> is canonical only up to a sixth root of unity. Safe to skip.
- 2-torsion. Several statements hold only modulo 2-torsion or after tensoring with ℚ. Safe to skip.
- Points not over ℚ. The P<sub>j</sub> may need a larger field, because E(ℚ) can be trivial. Safe to skip.
- Modularity. In 1995 this was a hypothesis. The theorem uses it only through Beilinson's result. Safe to skip.
