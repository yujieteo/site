## Before you start

This is a 71-page paper that asks you to accept new words (level, formation, diamond category) before it states its main theorem. The likely feeling is overwhelm, with some irritation at the vocabulary. Both are fair responses to the paper as written. The authors expect this and state the theorem first for finite groups, in one page. If you like the idea that a probability distribution on groups can be recovered from a few averages, that page is the whole pleasure. Enjoy it. The general machinery will keep.

## The story

A distribution on real numbers is often determined by its moments, the averages of X<sup>k</sup>. Number theorists want the same for random groups: class groups of random number fields, cokernels of random matrices, fundamental groups of random 3-manifolds. There the useful "moment" indexed by a finite group G is the average number of surjections from the random group onto G. Many papers proved that such moments determine the distribution, each in its own category, and each needed a candidate distribution in advance. Sawin and Wood remove both limits. For finitely many objects, the moment equations form a triangular matrix of epimorphism counts, and its inverse has a closed form given by Möbius functions of lattices of quotients. That inverse is a formula for the distribution. The work is in showing the infinite sums converge.

## Main definitions

**Diamond category (Definition 1.3).** A small category C such that the quotients of each object form a finite modular lattice, every automorphism group is finite, each object of a level has finitely many simple extensions within that level, and there are at most countably many minimal objects.
- Examples: finite groups, finite commutative rings, finite R-modules for any ring R.

**Level.** A set L of isomorphism classes generated by finitely many objects under quotients and joins of quotients (for groups: quotients and finite subdirect products). Each object X has a largest quotient X<sup>L</sup> in L.
- Example: ℤ/p generates the level of elementary abelian p-groups.

**Moment.** For a measure ν on isomorphism classes and an object G, the G-moment is ∫ |Epi(F, G)| dν(F), the average number of epimorphisms onto G.

**Well-behaved moments.** A family of real numbers (M<sub>G</sub>) is well-behaved at L if, for every F in L, the sum over G in L and epimorphisms π : G → F of |μ(F, G)| Z(π)<sup>3</sup> |M<sub>G</sub>| / |Aut(G)| is finite. Here μ is the Möbius function of the lattice of quotients of G, and Z(π) counts the elements between F and G that satisfy a distributive law. This is the "moments do not grow too fast" condition.

## Main results

**Theorem (distributions on a level; Theorem 1.6).** Let C be a diamond category, L a level, and (M<sub>G</sub>) well-behaved at L. Put v<sub>L,F</sub> = Σ<sub>G ∈ L</sub> μ̂(F, G) M<sub>G</sub> / |Aut(G)|, where μ̂(F, G) sums μ over epimorphisms G → F up to Aut(F). A measure on L with moments M<sub>G</sub> exists if and only if v<sub>L,F</sub> ≥ 0 for all F in L. It is then unique, and gives F the mass v<sub>L,F</sub>.
- Step 1: for finitely supported moments, the matrix of |Epi(G<sub>j</sub>, G<sub>i</sub>)| is triangular, and Lemma 4.1 gives its inverse as μ̂(G<sub>i</sub>, G<sub>j</sub>) / |Aut(G<sub>j</sub>)|.
- Step 2: in general, exchange sums one layer at a time along a chain of formations, where each layer involves only semisimple epimorphisms.

**Theorem (robustness; Theorems 1.6 and 1.8).** If measures ν<sup>t</sup> on L have G-moments that converge to M<sub>G</sub> for every G in L, then ν<sup>t</sup>({F}) → v<sub>L,F</sub> for every F. So convergence of moments forces convergence of distributions.

**Theorem (profinite version; Theorem 1.7).** Let P be the set of pro-isomorphism classes of C, with the sets U<sub>L,F</sub> = {X : X<sup>L</sup> ≅ F} generating the σ-algebra. For well-behaved (M<sub>G</sub>), a measure on P with moments M<sub>G</sub> exists if and only if all v<sub>L,F</sub> ≥ 0. It is unique, with ν(U<sub>L,F</sub>) = v<sub>L,F</sub>.
- Case of groups (Theorem 1.2): P is the set of profinite groups with finitely many continuous homomorphisms to each finite group.

## Note, then ignore

- The factor Z(π)<sup>3</sup>. The proof of existence needs it. The authors say they do not know whether the theorem needs it, and that it causes no trouble in their applications (Remark 1.9). Safe to skip on a first reading.
- Signed sums. The M<sub>G</sub> are any real numbers, and the formula for v<sub>L,F</sub> has alternating signs. This is why existence is a positivity condition. Safe to skip.
- Pro-objects. A pro-isomorphism class is a compatible family of objects X<sup>L</sup>, one per level. Lemma 5.7 matches these with "small" pro-objects. Safe to skip.
- Countability. Weak convergence in Theorem 1.8 assumes C has countably many isomorphism classes, so that the σ-algebra is the Borel one. Safe to skip.
- Surjections, not homomorphisms. For 𝔽<sub>q</sub>-vector spaces these moments are a triangular change of the classical moments of group size. Safe to skip.
