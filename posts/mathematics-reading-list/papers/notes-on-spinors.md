Nobody needs to finish the rest in one sitting.

## The story

Physicists need to know, for each dimension and signature, what spinors look like. Are they real, complex or quaternionic? Is there a pairing of two spinors into a vector, and is it symmetric? The answers were known but scattered, with a separate computation for each case. Deligne organises all of it by two periodicities modulo 8. The key idea is to treat the Clifford algebra as a super algebra, with a mod 2 grading, and to record its class in a super Brauer group. Over ℝ that group is cyclic of order 8, and the class of the Clifford algebra is the signature mod 8. So the reality type of spinors reads off from one table. A second table, in the dimension mod 8, gives the pairings.

## Main definitions

**Clifford algebra.** For a quadratic vector space (V, Q), the Clifford algebra C(V) is the associative algebra generated by V with relations x<sup>2</sup> = Q(x)·1. It is mod 2 graded with V odd. Its even part is C<sup>+</sup>(V).
- Orthogonal sums: C(V′ ⊕ V″) is the super tensor product of C(V′) and C(V″).

**Spinorial representation.** The Lie algebra so(V) maps into C<sup>+</sup>(V) by x∧y ↦ (xy − yx)/2. A spinorial representation is one that factors through C<sup>+</sup>(V). So spinorial representations are the same as C<sup>+</sup>(V)-modules.
- Over ℂ, even dimension: two simple ones, the semi-spin representations S<sup>+</sup> and S<sup>−</sup>.
- Over ℂ, odd dimension: one simple one, the spin representation S.

**Super Brauer group.** A super algebra over a field k of characteristic not 2 is central simple if, over the algebraic closure, it becomes a super matrix algebra or one tensored with D = k[ε], ε odd, ε<sup>2</sup> = 1. Two are similar if their simple modules have isomorphic commutants. Similarity classes under super tensor product form the group sBr(k).

## Main results

**Theorem (structure of complex Clifford algebras; Proposition 2.2).** Let V be a complex quadratic vector space. If dim V = 2n > 0, then C(V) ≅ End(S) for a super vector space S with even and odd parts each of dimension 2<sup>n−1</sup>. If dim V = 2n + 1, then C(V) ≅ D ⊗ M<sub>N</sub>(ℂ) with N = 2<sup>n</sup>.
- Step 1: write V = L ⊕ L<sup>∨</sup> with L isotropic, and let S be the exterior algebra of L<sup>∨</sup>. L<sup>∨</sup> acts by wedge product and L by contraction.
- Step 2: reduce to dim L = 1 by tensor products. For odd dimension, split off a line.

**Theorem (signature mod 8; Proposition 3.6, after Wall).** The super Brauer group of ℝ is cyclic of order 8, generated by the class of ℝ[ε] with ε odd and ε<sup>2</sup> = 1. For a real quadratic space V, C(V) is similar to the sgn(V)-th tensor power of ℝ[ε]. So the Morita class of C<sup>+</sup>(V) depends only on the signature mod 8.
- Step 1: sBr(k) is built from ℤ/2, k<sup>×</sup>/k<sup>×2</sup> and the ordinary Brauer group, so it has order 8 for k = ℝ.
- Step 2: an invariant made from signatures of trace forms sends ℝ[ε] to 1 + i, which has order 8 modulo positive reals.

**Theorem (reality of spinors; Table 1.4.1).** Let p − q be the signature. Irreducible real spinorial representations are: two real ones for p − q ≡ 0 mod 8, one real for 1 or 7, one complex for 2 or 6, one quaternionic for 3 or 5, two quaternionic for 4.

**Theorem (pairings over ℂ; Table 1.5.1).** For n odd, S is orthogonal for n ≡ 1, 7 mod 8 and symplectic for n ≡ 3, 5. The unique map S ⊗ S → V is symmetric for n ≡ 1, 3 and antisymmetric for n ≡ 5, 7. For n even, S<sup>+</sup> and S<sup>−</sup> are each orthogonal for n ≡ 0, each symplectic for n ≡ 4, and dual to each other for n ≡ 2, 6.
- Case n ≡ 0, 4: the pairing into V is S<sup>+</sup> ⊗ S<sup>−</sup> → V.
- Case n ≡ 2, 6: there are maps S<sup>±</sup> ⊗ S<sup>±</sup> → V, symmetric for 2 and antisymmetric for 6.

## Note, then ignore

- Sign convention. Deligne follows Bourbaki with x<sup>2</sup> = Q(x). Many authors use −Q(x). This swaps signature p − q with q − p in other sources. Safe to skip on a first reading.
- "Unique" means up to scalar. Uniqueness of a morphism is always projective here. Safe to skip.
- Super versus ungraded. The principal antiautomorphism reverses products in the ungraded sense, not the super sense. It matters only when you check signs in Section 4. Safe to skip.
- Real points of Spin. Over a general field, Spin(k) → SO(k) need not be surjective. This does not affect the tables. Safe to skip.
- Heisenberg analogy. Sections 2.3 and 2.5 compare spinors with the oscillator representation. It is a side remark. Safe to skip.
