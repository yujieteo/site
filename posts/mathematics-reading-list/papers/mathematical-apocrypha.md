## Before you start

This page may raise a small guilt: it is gossip, not mathematics, and reading it can feel like skipping work. That feeling is allowed. Nothing here needs to be learned or kept. If you want to know what the people behind the theorems were like in a seminar room, this page gives you that in a few minutes. Read it for fun. If you stop after three stories, that is a complete reading.

## The story

Textbooks give you theorems with the people removed. Milne's page puts some of the people back. It holds ten short anecdotes with no headings and no commentary. Most happen in seminars at the Institute for Advanced Study, Harvard or the Bourbaki seminar. A newcomer asks Borel and Langlands a question and gets a dry answer. Grothendieck presses Zariski to prove a result for all schemes, and Zariski answers with a counterexample. Tate tells a student that the student's real mistake was to believe his own counterexample. Weil asks awkward questions about the Monster group. A closing note says where each story comes from, so you can judge how far to trust it. The title already warns you: apocrypha are stories of doubtful authority.

## Main definitions

**Apocrypha.** Stories whose truth nobody has checked against records. Milne uses the word for the whole page, and the closing note grades each story by how he came to know it.
- Witnessed: three stories carry the note that Milne was present.
- Passed on: the others came from Borel, from Messing, from fellow graduate students, or are marked hearsay or folklore.

**The Artin–Tate formula (as the page uses it).** A conjectured formula of Tate and Michael Artin for algebraic surfaces over finite fields. The page gives no statement of it. It only says that Tate set a thesis problem about it.

**The Monster.** A finite simple group of one particular very large order. At the time of the Bourbaki talk in the story, the speaker said its existence was not yet known.

## Main results

**Claim (general beats special, until it does not; third and fourth stories).** At Harvard, Grothendieck kept asking Zariski why a result was not proved for all schemes. Zariski said it fails, and produced a counterexample when Grothendieck tried a proof at the board.
- Case 1: the general statement is false, and the counterexample settles it on the spot.
- Case 2: at a later lecture the same question comes back, and Zariski asks for self-control.

**Claim (trust the structure; seventh story).** A student, who appears to be Milne himself, computed two sides of the Artin–Tate formula and got p<sup>13</sup> on one side and p<sup>17</sup> on the other. He took this as a counterexample. While explaining it to Tate, he found his own error.
- Step 1: Tate was pleased to hear of a possible counterexample, and asked to see it.
- Step 2: Tate's verdict was that the student ought to have assumed an error, because the theory was too well made to be false.

**Claim (a conjecture can be wrong only at 2; eighth story).** In a Harvard seminar someone said a conjecture of Lichtenbaum was false, because the power of 2 came out wrong in the only tested case. Tate answered from the audience that it was wrong only for 2. The page adds that the conjecture is probably correct apart from the power of 2.

**Claim (knowledge is not uniform; fifth story).** According to Messing, Grothendieck did not know the usual formula that expresses ∫ exp(−x<sup>2</sup>) dx through π, and thought he had never seen it.

## Note, then ignore

- Reliability. The sources note separates witnessed stories from hearsay, because the two deserve different trust. Safe to skip on a first reading.
- The time machine. The last story has Weil travel ten years ahead and copy out a proof of the Riemann hypothesis. The note labels it Fine Hall folklore, so it is a joke and not a report. Safe to skip.
- No dates. The page gives no years for the events, because it is a set of recollections. Safe to skip.
- The narrator. The thesis story is told in the first person and the page never names the speaker. The sources note points to Milne. Safe to skip.
- No mathematics stated. The page names the Artin–Tate formula and Lichtenbaum's conjecture and states neither, because the stories do not need them. Safe to skip.
