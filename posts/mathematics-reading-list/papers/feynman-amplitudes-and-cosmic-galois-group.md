## Before you start

These are 68 pages of lecture notes, and the introduction already speaks of motives, Tannaka groups and blow-ups. That can bring overwhelm, mixed with a doubt that an engineer is allowed in this room. Both feelings can sit there while you read. If you have wondered why ζ(3) and ζ(5) keep turning up in particle physics, this is where the question gets a structure. Section 0.1 is written for physicists and uses only the dilogarithm. Read that for pleasure. The rest is optional.

## The story

A Feynman graph gives an integral, and the integral is a period: a number or function obtained by integrating an algebraic form over a domain cut out by inequalities. Functions such as the dilogarithm Li<sub>2</sub>(z) carry a coproduct that records their differential equations and monodromy. At z = 1 the function collapses to the number ζ(2), and that structure seems lost. Brown recovers it by replacing each integral with a motivic period, a symbol that remembers the geometry it came from. Symbols carry a coaction, which is the same as the action of a group. Panzer and Schnetz observed by computer that amplitudes in massless φ<sup>4</sup> theory are closed under this action. Brown sets out to explain why. His key tool is a factorisation of graph polynomials, which makes the boundary of each graph's geometry a product of the geometries of smaller graphs.

## Main definitions

**Feynman integral, type (Q, M).** For a Feynman graph G, the integral I<sub>G</sub>(q, m) is the integral over the simplex σ<sub>G</sub> = {all α<sub>e</sub> ≥ 0} of a form ω<sub>G</sub> = P(α)Ω<sub>G</sub> / (Ψ<sub>G</sub><sup>A</sup> Ξ<sub>G</sub>(q, m)<sup>B</sup>). There is one variable α<sub>e</sub> per internal edge, and Ψ<sub>G</sub>, Ξ<sub>G</sub> are the graph polynomials. A graph has type (Q, M) if it has at most Q external kinematic parameters and at most M non-zero masses.

**Category of realisations H(S).** For a smooth scheme S over ℚ, an object is a triple (V<sub>B</sub>, V<sub>dR</sub>, c). V<sub>B</sub> is a local system of ℚ-vector spaces on S(ℂ). V<sub>dR</sub> is an algebraic vector bundle with integrable connection and regular singularities. c is a comparison between them. The data must define a variation of mixed Hodge structures.

**Motivic Feynman integral.** The symbol I<sup>m</sup><sub>G</sub> = [mot<sub>G</sub>, σ<sub>G</sub>, ω<sub>G</sub>]<sup>m</sup>, a matrix coefficient of the object mot<sub>G</sub> in H(S). The period map sends it back to I<sub>G</sub>(q, m).

**Cosmic Galois group C<sub>Q,M</sub>.** The quotient of the Tannaka group of H(S), taken with the de Rham fibre functor at the generic point, that acts faithfully on the motivic periods of all mot<sub>G</sub> for G of type (Q, M). The name is Cartier's.
- Equivalent form: a coaction Δ from the space FP<sup>m</sup><sub>Q,M</sub> of such motivic periods to FP<sup>m</sup><sub>Q,M</sub> ⊗ FP<sup>dr</sup><sub>Q,M</sub>, where the second factor is the ring of de Rham periods.

## Main results

**Theorem (canonical motive of a graph; Theorem 0.1).** Let G be a Feynman graph with generic kinematics and convergent integral. Then there are, canonically, an object mot<sub>G</sub> in H(S) with S a Zariski open set in a space of kinematics, a de Rham class [ω<sub>G</sub>] and a Betti class [σ<sub>G</sub>], such that σ<sub>G</sub>(c(ω<sub>G</sub>)) = I<sub>G</sub>(q, m).
- Step 1: blow up projective space along linear subspaces to get P<sup>G</sup>, so that the simplex no longer meets the singularities badly.
- Step 2: take the cohomology of the pair (P<sup>G</sup> minus Y<sub>G</sub>, D minus D ∩ Y<sub>G</sub>), where Y<sub>G</sub> is the strict transform of the zero loci of Ψ<sub>G</sub> and Ξ<sub>G</sub> and D is a normal crossing divisor.

**Theorem (finiteness in bounded weight; Theorem 0.2).** The weight-at-most-k part W<sub>k</sub>FP<sup>m</sup><sub>Q,M</sub> is finite-dimensional. So for each fixed type (Q, M), the convergent Feynman integrals of bounded weight span a finite-dimensional space, although there are infinitely many graphs.
- Step 1: the partial factorisation Ψ<sub>G</sub> = Ψ<sub>γ</sub>Ψ<sub>G/γ</sub> + R, and its two analogues for Ξ<sub>G</sub>, make each open boundary stratum a product of varieties of the same kind.
- Step 2: hence the weight-graded pieces of mot<sub>G</sub> in weight at most k come from minors of G with at most k + 1 edges. The precise form is Theorem 8.11: affine motivic periods of G of weight at most k are combinations of those of finitely many "motic descendents" of G.

**Conjecture (small graphs principle; Conjecture 1).** The motivic periods of G of weight at most k lie in the algebra generated by regularised motivic periods of graph minors of G with at most k + 1 edges. Brown proves a weaker version with "affine" in place of "regularised". A suitable notion of regularised motivic period is what is missing.

## Note, then ignore

- Not Connes and Marcolli's group. They defined a cosmic Galois group for renormalisation. Brown says it is unclear whether the two are related. Safe to skip.
- "Motive" is used loosely. Brown works in H(S) to avoid conjectures about true motives. Safe to skip on a first reading.
- Amplitude versus full amplitude. Here "amplitude" means one graph's integral with a specific numerator, not the sum over all graphs at a loop order. Keep it in mind, then move on.
- Not a Hopf algebra. The coaction is one-sided: motivic periods map to numbers, de Rham periods do not. Safe to skip.
- Even dimensions only. The construction covers graphs in an even number of space-time dimensions. Safe to skip.
