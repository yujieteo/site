The likely feeling is irritation, and under it a doubt that classical special functions are worth the time. Both are reasonable. If you have met F(a, b, c | z) in engineering and want to know why it keeps turning up, Chapter 2 answers with pictures of triangles. Read for the pictures first. Covering all four chapters is optional.

## The story

Gauss's series F(a, b, c | z) solves a second order equation with singular points only at 0, 1 and ∞. Continue a pair of solutions around a singular point and they return as a linear combination of themselves. These matrices form the monodromy group. People wanted it in terms of a, b, c, and direct analytic continuation is messy. The idea here is that you hardly need it. Near z = 1 all but one solution is holomorphic, so the loop around 1 acts as a reflection. Levelt's theorem says two matrices with given eigenvalues whose quotient is a reflection are unique up to conjugation. So the local exponents decide everything. You get clean tests for when the group is reducible, unitary or finite, and finite means that the function is algebraic.

## Main definitions

**Fuchsian equation (Definition 1.3.1).** A linear differential equation over ℂ(z) is Fuchsian if every point of the projective line is a regular point or a regular singularity. At a regular singularity P the coefficient p<sub>i</sub> has a pole of order at most i.

**Hypergeometric equation.** With D = z d/dz, the equation z(D + α<sub>1</sub>)⋯(D + α<sub>n</sub>)F = (D + β<sub>1</sub> − 1)⋯(D + β<sub>n</sub> − 1)F. It is Fuchsian of order n with singularities at 0, 1, ∞.
- Exponents at 0: the numbers 1 − β<sub>i</sub>. At ∞: the numbers α<sub>i</sub>.
- Case n = 2: Gauss's equation z(z − 1)F″ + ((a + b + 1)z − c)F′ + abF = 0.

**Hypergeometric group (Definition 3.3.1).** Given nonzero complex a<sub>1</sub>, …, a<sub>n</sub> and b<sub>1</sub>, …, b<sub>n</sub> with a<sub>i</sub> ≠ b<sub>j</sub>, the group generated by matrices A and B with these eigenvalues such that AB<sup>−1</sup> − 1 has rank one. The monodromy group of the equation is of this kind, with a<sub>k</sub> = e<sup>2πiα<sub>k</sub></sup> and b<sub>k</sub> = e<sup>2πiβ<sub>k</sub></sup>.

**Interlacing (Definition 3.3.3).** Two n-point sets on the unit circle interlace if their points alternate as you go round the circle.

## Main results

**Theorem (Schwarz; Theorem 2.2.12).** Let a, b, c be real, and put λ = |1 − c|, μ = |c − a − b|, ν = |a − b|. Suppose all three are less than 1. Then the quotient of two independent solutions of Gauss's equation maps the closed upper half-plane one-to-one onto a triangle bounded by circular arcs, with angles λπ, μπ, νπ at the images of 0, 1, ∞.
- Step 1: the quotient is locally injective, because the Wronskian has no zeros in the upper half-plane.
- Step 2: each of the three real intervals has a real basis of solutions, so its image lies on a circle or line.

**Theorem (Levelt; Theorem 3.2.3).** Let a<sub>1</sub>, …, a<sub>n</sub> and b<sub>1</sub>, …, b<sub>n</sub> be nonzero complex numbers with a<sub>i</sub> ≠ b<sub>j</sub> for all i, j. Then matrices A, B in GL(n, ℂ) with these eigenvalues and AB<sup>−1</sup> a reflection exist, and the pair is unique up to simultaneous conjugation.
- Existence: use the companion matrices of ∏(X − a<sub>i</sub>) and ∏(X − b<sub>i</sub>).
- Consequence (Corollary 3.2.2): the monodromy acts irreducibly exactly when no difference α<sub>i</sub> − β<sub>j</sub> is an integer.

**Theorem (unitary and finite monodromy; Corollary 3.3.4 and Theorem 3.3.5).** Suppose all parameters a<sub>i</sub>, b<sub>j</sub> lie on the unit circle. The hypergeometric group lies in the unitary group U(n) if and only if the two sets interlace. If the parameters are h-th roots of unity, the group is finite if and only if the k-th powers of the two sets interlace for every k prime to h.
- Step 1: the group always preserves a nondegenerate Hermitian form (Theorem 3.3.2), which is definite exactly in the interlacing case.
- Step 2: Galois conjugation carries the group to the groups with k-th power parameters, and bounded conjugates of cyclotomic integers are finite in number.

**Theorem (Katz, rigidity; Theorem 3.4.1).** Let g<sub>1</sub>, …, g<sub>r</sub> in GL(n, k) generate an irreducible group with g<sub>1</sub>⋯g<sub>r</sub> = 1. Let δ<sub>i</sub> be the codimension of the centraliser of g<sub>i</sub> in the n × n matrices. Then Σδ<sub>i</sub> ≥ 2(n<sup>2</sup> − 1). If equality holds, the tuple is determined up to simultaneous conjugation by the conjugacy classes of the g<sub>i</sub>.

## Note, then ignore

- Reflections. The notes say "(pseudo)reflection" for any h with h − 1 of rank one. No finite order is assumed. Safe to skip.
- Wrong equation labels. In Chapters 2 and 3 the text often cites "(4.1)" where it means the hypergeometric equation of that chapter. Read through it.
- Real parameters. Chapter 2 assumes a, b, c real so that eigenvalues lie on the unit circle. The reducibility test (one of a, b, c − a, c − b is an integer) does not need it. Safe to skip.
- Chapter 1. Picard–Vessiot and Riemann–Hilbert are background. The later chapters use mainly local exponents. Skim on a first reading.
- Chapter 4. It finds explicit monodromy matrices by Mellin–Barnes integrals. Levelt's theorem already gives the group up to conjugation. Safe to skip.
