## Before you start

These are talk slides, about 25 of them, with little prose between the formulas. The likely feeling is mild frustration: you can see each slide but not the voice that joined them. That is a fair thing to feel with slides, and nothing is wrong with you if a step looks abrupt. If you want to see why Coxeter diagrams exist at all, this talk shows it with squares and cubes. Enjoy the pictures of the square first. The classification table can wait.

## The story

In the plane there is a regular m-gon for every m ≥ 3. In space there are five regular solids. What happens in dimension n, and why does a short string of integers, the Schläfli symbol {m<sub>1</sub>, …, m<sub>n−1</sub>}, pin the shape down? Vogan borrows an idea from linear algebra: study flags. In a polyhedron a flag is a chain vertex ⊂ edge ⊂ face ⊂ …. Regular means the symmetry group moves any flag to any other. Each flag has exactly one neighbour that differs in the i-dimensional face only, and the symmetry that swaps the two is a reflection s<sub>i</sub>. For a square, s<sub>0</sub> and s<sub>1</sub> generate all eight symmetries. The orders of the rotations s<sub>k</sub>s<sub>k+1</sub> are the Schläfli numbers. Coordinates built from centres of mass then turn "does this symbol occur?" into a positivity test.

## Main definitions

**Flag.** Let P be a compact convex polyhedron of dimension n. A complete flag is a chain P<sub>0</sub> ⊂ P<sub>1</sub> ⊂ … ⊂ P<sub>n</sub> = P with dim P<sub>i</sub> = i and each P<sub>i−1</sub> a face of P<sub>i</sub>.
- Adjacent flags: a flag is i-adjacent to F if it differs from F in the i-dimensional member only. There is exactly one such flag for each i from 0 to n−1, with no symmetry assumed.

**Regular polyhedron.** P is regular if its symmetry group acts transitively on complete flags.
- Non-example: the slides draw a plane figure whose symmetry group, generated by the reflections in the two axes, is transitive on edges but not on flags.

**Basic reflections.** For a regular P with a fixed flag F, s<sub>i</sub> is the unique symmetry that sends F to its i-adjacent flag. Write p<sub>j</sub> for the centre of mass of P<sub>j</sub>. Then s<sub>i</sub> has order 2 and is the orthogonal reflection in the hyperplane through all the p<sub>j</sub> with j ≠ i.

**Schläfli symbol.** The string {m<sub>1</sub>, …, m<sub>n−1</sub>}, where m<sub>k+1</sub> is the order of the rotation s<sub>k</sub>s<sub>k+1</sub>. So m<sub>1</sub> says the 2-faces are m<sub>1</sub>-gons, and m<sub>2</sub> counts the 2-faces of a fixed 3-face that meet at a fixed vertex.

## Main results

**Theorem (a fixed flag fixes everything; the slides call it a Lemma, unnumbered).** An affine map that preserves a complete flag of a compact convex polyhedron P<sub>n</sub> acts trivially on P<sub>n</sub>.
- Step 1: induct on n. The map fixes the centre of mass p<sub>n</sub> and, by induction, the affine span of P<sub>n−1</sub>.
- Step 2: p<sub>n</sub> lies outside that span, so together they span everything.

**Theorem (one symmetry per flag; unnumbered).** For a regular P with fixed flag F and any complete flag G, exactly one symmetry w satisfies wF = G. So the symmetries are in bijection with the flags.

**Theorem (relations; unnumbered).** The reflections s<sub>0</sub>, …, s<sub>n−1</sub> satisfy s<sub>k</sub><sup>2</sup> = 1, s<sub>k</sub>s<sub>k′</sub> = s<sub>k′</sub>s<sub>k</sub> when |k − k′| > 1, and (s<sub>k</sub>s<sub>k+1</sub>)<sup>m<sub>k+1</sub></sup> = 1 with m<sub>k+1</sub> ≥ 3. The slides state these for the cube as a full presentation of its symmetry group, the permutations and sign changes of coordinates.
- Step 1: put p<sub>n</sub> at the origin and rotate so that p<sub>k</sub> = (a<sub>n</sub>, …, a<sub>k+1</sub>, 0, …, 0) with every a<sub>i</sub> > 0.
- Step 2: the shape is then fixed by the n−1 positive ratios r<sub>k</sub> = (a<sub>k+1</sub>/a<sub>k</sub>)<sup>2</sup>, and the rotation angle θ<sub>k+1</sub> = 2π/m<sub>k+1</sub> of s<sub>k</sub>s<sub>k+1</sub> is an explicit function of r<sub>k</sub> and r<sub>k+1</sub>.

**Theorem (classification; final table, credited to Coxeter).** A symbol occurs exactly when the recursion that solves for the r<sub>k</sub> from the cos(2π/m<sub>k</sub>) gives positive numbers. The first condition is cos θ<sub>1</sub> + cos θ<sub>2</sub> &lt; 0, which forces one of two neighbouring m's to be 3 and the other to be at most 5. The table lists the outcomes: the n-simplex, the hypercube and hyperoctahedron, the m-gons, the icosahedron and dodecahedron (group of order 120), the 600-cell and 120-cell (order 14400) and the 24-cell (order 1152).
- The slides give the idea and the table, not a full proof.

## Note, then ignore

- Linear algebra warm-up. The slides on Borel and parabolic subgroups of GL(V) are the model for the flag idea. They are not used in the proofs. Safe to skip on a first reading.
- Reflections without inner products. One slide defines a reflection by a vector and a covector, with no orthogonality, and computes the order of a product from the numbers c<sub>st</sub>c<sub>ts</sub>. The polyhedron case only needs orthogonal reflections. Safe to skip.
- Compactness. The fixed-flag statement fails for the half-line [0, ∞). The hypothesis is there for that reason only. Safe to skip.
- Dimensions 0 and 1. The point and the interval count as regular, and the induction even starts from the empty set in dimension −1. This is bookkeeping. Safe to skip.
- Dual pairs. One row of the table can hold two solids, such as the cube and the octahedron, because reading the symbol backwards gives the same group. Safe to skip.
