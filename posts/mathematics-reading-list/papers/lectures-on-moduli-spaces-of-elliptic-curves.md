## Before you start

These are 80 pages of lecture notes with over 100 exercises, and the words "orbifold" and "stack" appear in the abstract. The likely feeling is a fear of being too slow: that each exercise is a toll gate and the stacks at the end are out of reach. It is fine to feel that. If you want to know what a moduli space is by holding one in your hands, this is the smallest complete example, and the first section needs only lattices in ℂ. Read for the picture. Do an exercise when one looks like fun.

## The story

One wants a space M whose points are the isomorphism classes of elliptic curves, such that a family of elliptic curves over a base T is the same thing as a map T → M. Points are easy. Every elliptic curve is ℂ modulo a lattice, the lattice can be scaled to have basis 1 and τ, and changing the basis moves τ by SL<sub>2</sub>(ℤ). So the set of classes is SL<sub>2</sub>(ℤ)\h, a Riemann surface. Families are the obstacle. Every curve has the automorphism −1, and two special curves have more, so non-trivial families exist whose map to the quotient is constant. The cure is to stop forgetting the group. Keep the pair (h, SL<sub>2</sub>(ℤ)) as an orbifold M<sub>1,1</sub>. Then families and maps match, modular forms become sections of line bundles, and the Picard group and homology can be computed.

## Main definitions

**Elliptic curve, framing.** An elliptic curve is a compact Riemann surface X of genus 1 with a chosen point P. A framing is an ordered basis a, b of H<sub>1</sub>(X, ℤ) with intersection number a · b = 1.
- Framed curves up to isomorphism correspond to points τ of the upper half plane h, through the lattice with basis 1, τ.

**The orbifold M<sub>1,1</sub> (Definition 3.12).** The orbifold quotient of h by SL<sub>2</sub>(ℤ): the pair of the space and the group action, not only the set of orbits. Its orbifold fundamental group is SL<sub>2</sub>(ℤ). The ordinary quotient Riemann surface SL<sub>2</sub>(ℤ)\h is its coarse moduli space.
- Compactification: glue in a disc with coordinate q = exp(2πiτ) and trivial action of the group of order 2. This adds one point ∞ and gives M̄<sub>1,1</sub>.

**The line bundles L<sub>k</sub> and the Hodge bundle.** L<sub>k</sub> is the quotient of ℂ × h by SL<sub>2</sub>(ℤ), where a matrix with bottom row (c, d) sends (z, τ) to ((cτ + d)<sup>k</sup> z, γτ). The Hodge bundle has as fibre over a curve its space of holomorphic 1-forms.
- Modular forms of weight k are the holomorphic sections of the extension of L<sub>k</sub> to M̄<sub>1,1</sub>.

## Main results

**Theorem (M<sub>1,1</sub> classifies families; Theorem 3.18).** For a complex manifold T, isomorphism classes of families of elliptic curves over T correspond one to one to holomorphic orbifold morphisms T → M<sub>1,1</sub>. The morphism is the period mapping, and the family is the pullback of the universal curve.
- Step 1: framed families over T correspond to holomorphic maps T → h (Proposition 2.4).
- Step 2: every family can be framed locally, and the choices of framing are absorbed by the SL<sub>2</sub>(ℤ) in the orbifold structure.

**Theorem (modular forms; Proposition 4.5 and Theorem 5.15).** The ring of modular forms for SL<sub>2</sub>(ℤ) is freely generated by the Eisenstein series G<sub>4</sub> and G<sub>6</sub>. The cusp forms are the ideal generated by Δ. The Hodge bundle over M̄<sub>1,1</sub> is isomorphic to L = L<sub>1</sub>.

**Theorem (Picard groups; Theorems 6.6 and 6.9).** Pic M<sub>1,1</sub> is cyclic of order 12, generated by the class of L. Pic M̄<sub>1,1</sub> is infinite cyclic, generated by the Hodge bundle L. For every point P of the coarse space of M̄<sub>1,1</sub>, the class of O(P) equals 12[L].
- Topology behind it: H<sub>1</sub>(M<sub>1,1</sub>; ℤ) = ℤ/12ℤ, H<sup>1</sup> = 0 and H<sup>2</sup>(M<sub>1,1</sub>; ℤ) = ℤ/12ℤ (Proposition 3.13).

**Theorem (stable reduction; Theorem 5.9).** Let X be a family of smooth elliptic curves over a Riemann surface T minus finitely many points. The coarse period map extends to a holomorphic map from T to the compactified coarse space. After a finite covering of T unramified over the punctured part, the period mapping itself extends to a morphism into M̄<sub>1,1</sub>.

## Note, then ignore

- Two kinds of backslash. The notes write Γ\X for the ordinary quotient and Γ\\X for the orbifold. The difference is the whole point, but the notation settles in quickly. Safe to skip on a first reading.
- Starred sections. Hain marks sections outside the main line with an asterisk, for example level structures in Section 4.2. Safe to skip.
- Isotrivial families. Non-trivial families with constant coarse period map, such as quadratic twists, are the reason Theorem 3.18 needs orbifold maps. Note the example and move on.
- Stacks. Appendix B is a very brief sketch. The orbifold language carries all the main results. Safe to skip.
- Normalisations. The cubic is written y<sup>2</sup> = 4x<sup>3</sup> − g<sub>2</sub>x − g<sub>3</sub> with g<sub>2</sub> = 60G<sub>4</sub> and g<sub>3</sub> = 140G<sub>6</sub>. Other books scale these differently. Safe to skip.
