## The story

Classical modular forms live on the upper half-plane and come with Hecke operators T<sub>p</sub>, newforms and L-functions. Automorphic representations of GL<sub>2</sub> over the adeles come with local components π<sub>p</sub>, one for each prime and one for the real place. People wanted an exact translation between the two. The blocks are bookkeeping and one real question. The bookkeeping is normalisation: the two Hecke operators differ by a power of p, and the two L-functions differ by a shift in s. The real question is whether the representation π<sub>f</sub> generated by an eigenform f is irreducible. The answer is yes. All pieces of π<sub>f</sub> share the same Hecke eigenvalues at almost all primes and the same weight at infinity, and Strong Multiplicity One then forces them to be one piece. Newforms then turn out to be the distinguished vectors picked out by the conductor.

## Main definitions

**Classical Hecke operator.** For f of weight k, level Γ<sub>0</sub>(N) and character χ, the operator T<sub>p</sub> is p<sup>k/2−1</sup> times the sum of the slash actions of the coset representatives of the double coset of diag(1, p), each weighted by the character. On q-expansions it sends Σ a<sub>n</sub>q<sup>n</sup> to Σ b<sub>n</sub>q<sup>n</sup> with b<sub>n</sub> = a<sub>np</sub> + χ(p)p<sup>k−1</sup>a<sub>n/p</sub>.

**Adelic lift and adelic Hecke operator.** The lift of f is the function φ<sub>f</sub>(g) = f|<sub>k,g</sub>(i) on the adelic group. The operator T̃<sub>p</sub> is convolution with the indicator function of the double coset K<sub>p</sub> diag(p, 1) K<sub>p</sub>, where K<sub>p</sub> = GL<sub>2</sub>(ℤ<sub>p</sub>).
- It is purely local: it acts only on the component π<sub>p</sub>.

**Newform.** Oldforms of level N are spanned by f(z) and f(dz) for forms f of lower level M with dM = N. Newforms are the orthogonal complement of the oldforms under the Petersson inner product.

**Conductor.** For an admissible infinite-dimensional representation π of GL<sub>2</sub>(F), with F a non-archimedean local field and ω the central character, cond(π) is the largest ideal such that some non-zero vector v satisfies π(g)v = ω(a)v for all g in Γ<sub>0</sub>(cond(π)), where a is the upper left entry of g.
- Existence and uniqueness: such an ideal exists and the space of such v is 1-dimensional (Theorem 2.9, Casselman and Novodvorskii).
- Global: the conductor of an automorphic representation is the product of the local conductors.

## Main results

**Lemma (Hecke operators match; Lemma 1.14).** For a modular form f of weight k, p<sup>k/2−1</sup> T̃<sub>p</sub>(φ<sub>f</sub>) = φ<sub>T<sub>p</sub>f</sub>. The notes leave the proof as an exercise.

**Theorem (irreducibility; Theorem 2.2).** Let f be a cusp form of weight k, level Γ<sub>0</sub>(N) and character χ that is an eigenform for almost all Hecke operators. Then the automorphic representation π<sub>f</sub> generated by f is irreducible.
- Step 1: the closure of π<sub>f</sub> splits as a Hilbert direct sum of irreducibles π<sub>i</sub>. Each π<sub>i</sub> has a spherical vector with eigenvalue a<sub>p</sub>(f) at almost all p, and a spherical representation is determined by its central character and this eigenvalue (Corollary 1.17).
- Step 2: at infinity each π<sub>i</sub> has a weight k vector killed by the lowering operator, so it is the discrete series of weight k. Strong Multiplicity One and Multiplicity One then make all π<sub>i</sub> the same space.

**Theorem (classical multiplicity one; Theorem 2.7).** Let f and f′ be newforms of weight k for Γ<sub>0</sub>(N) that are Hecke eigenforms with a<sub>p</sub>(f) = a<sub>p</sub>(f′) for almost all p. Then f and f′ are proportional.
- Step 1: in each cuspidal representation, take the lowest weight vector at infinity and the conductor vector at each prime. This distinguished vector is a newform, and it is the only one in the representation (Proposition 2.15).
- Step 2: apply Strong Multiplicity One to the two representations.

**Theorem (local components; Theorem 2.20).** Let N be a product of distinct primes and f a newform of weight k for Γ<sub>0</sub>(N) with trivial character and T<sub>p</sub>f = a<sub>p</sub>f for all p. Then π<sub>∞</sub> is the discrete series of weight k. For p not dividing N, π<sub>p</sub> is the unramified principal series π(μ<sub>1</sub>, μ<sub>2</sub>) with μ<sub>1</sub>μ<sub>2</sub> = 1 and a<sub>p</sub> = p<sup>(k−1)/2</sup>(p<sup>s<sub>1</sub></sup> + p<sup>s<sub>2</sub></sup>). For p dividing N, π<sub>p</sub> is a special representation with trivial central character.
- Why: the conductor at p dividing N is the maximal ideal. Supercuspidals have conductor exponent at least 2, and principal series with trivial central character have even exponent.

## Note, then ignore

- Central character. With these conventions the adelic central character ω corresponds to χ<sup>−1</sup>, not χ (Remark 2.1). It is a convention, safe to skip on a first reading.
- L-function shift. Corollary 2.17 proves L(f, s) = L(π<sub>f</sub>, s − (k−1)/2) in level one, while Remark 2.18 writes the general statement without the shift. The difference is normalisation, safe to skip.
- T<sub>p</sub> is normal, not self-adjoint. Theorem 1.8 gives (T<sub>p</sub>f, g) = χ(p)(f, T<sub>p</sub>g) for p prime to N, which is enough to diagonalise. Safe to skip.
- Kirillov model. The supercuspidal case of Theorem 2.9 uses a model quoted from Jacquet and Langlands. Only that case needs it, so it is safe to skip.
