slogan: One blow-up of projective 3-space is both a quotient of the Siegel half space and a quotient of the complex 3-ball, with different boundaries.

## Before you start

This is a 40-page paper from 1990, full of counts of planes, lines and cusps. The feeling it may raise is boredom at lists of configurations, mixed with a worry that you are missing the point. Both are reasonable here, and you can let them sit. If you enjoy seeing one concrete space carry two different symmetric structures, this is a rare place where you can hold the whole example in your hand. Start with the picture of the tetrahedron and enjoy it. The rest is optional.

## The story

Take a bounded symmetric domain 𝔻 and an arithmetic group Γ acting on it. If the quotient Γ\𝔻 is compact, its Chern numbers recover 𝔻, so one variety cannot be a quotient of two different domains. Most moduli spaces are not compact. Then you study a smooth variety X with a divisor D such that X − D = Γ\𝔻. The question is whether one X can carry two such structures, with two different divisors and two different domains. Hunt shows it can. Blow up ℙ<sup>3</sup> along a classical arrangement of ten planes. Remove one set of divisors and you get a Siegel modular threefold of level 2. Remove another set and you get a ball quotient. The reason is a moduli coincidence: six points on a line give both a genus 2 curve y<sup>2</sup> = p<sub>6</sub>(x) and a genus 4 curve y<sup>3</sup> = p<sub>6</sub>(x).

## Main definitions

**Locally symmetric variety.** A smooth algebraic variety X that contains a quotient Γ\𝔻 as a Zariski-open subset, where 𝔻 is a bounded symmetric domain and Γ a discrete group of finite covolume. With a divisor D such that X − D = Γ\𝔻, Hunt says that the pair (X, D) corresponds to the pair (𝔻, Γ), and then Γ = π<sub>1</sub>(X − D).

**The variety X.** Start with ℙ<sup>3</sup> and ten planes: the 4 coordinate planes, which form a tetrahedron, and the 6 symmetry planes of that tetrahedron. Blow up the 4 corners and the centre of the tetrahedron, then blow up along the 10 lines where three of the planes meet.
- D is the sum of 15 divisors: the 10 planes and the 5 exceptional copies of ℙ<sup>2</sup> over the blown-up points.
- E is the sum of 10 divisors: the exceptional copies of ℙ<sup>1</sup> × ℙ<sup>1</sup> over the 10 lines.

**The two groups.** Γ(2) is the principal congruence subgroup of level 2 in Sp(4, ℤ), acting on the Siegel upper half space 𝕊<sub>2</sub> of degree 2. Γ(1 − ρ) is the principal congruence subgroup for the ideal (1 − ρ) in the unitary group U(3,1) over the ring of integers of K = ℚ(√−3), with ρ a primitive cube root of unity. It acts on the complex 3-ball 𝔹<sub>3</sub>.

**Picard curve (in this paper).** A curve y<sup>3</sup> = p<sub>6</sub>(x) with p<sub>6</sub> a sextic. It has genus 4 and an action of ℤ/3. The classical Picard curves, y<sup>3</sup> equal to a quartic, have genus 3, and Hunt keeps the name for the genus 4 analogue.

## Main results

**Theorem (two structures on X; Theorem in the Introduction).** Let X, D, E be as above.
- Case (i): (X, D) corresponds to (𝕊<sub>2</sub>, Γ(2)). So X − D is the Siegel modular threefold of level 2.
- Case (ii): (X, E) corresponds to (𝔹<sub>3</sub>, Γ(1 − ρ)). So X − E is a Picard modular threefold.

**Claim (moduli meaning; Introduction).** The open set X − D − E parametrises genus 2 curves y<sup>2</sup> = p<sub>6</sub>(x) and also genus 4 Picard curves y<sup>3</sup> = p<sub>6</sub>(x). The match sends each curve to the six zeros of p<sub>6</sub>. I read this only in the introduction. The proof uses a result of Shimura on moduli of Picard curves.

**Claim (dual degenerations; Introduction, table 5.4).** The two families degenerate in opposite ways when zeros of p<sub>6</sub> collide.
- Genus 2 curves: along D the curve gets a double point, and along E it splits into two components.
- Picard curves: the roles of D and E are exchanged.

**Claim (hypergeometric equations; Introduction).** The periods of both families satisfy Picard–Fuchs equations of hypergeometric type, with different parameters. Hunt takes the ball side from Deligne and Mostow, and the Siegel side from Sasaki and Yoshida. He also states that the Tits buildings of the two groups are dual to each other, and says it is not clear whether the double structure forces this.

## Note, then ignore

- Notation Sp(2, ℤ): Hunt writes Sp(2, ·) for the group of 4 × 4 symplectic matrices, which many authors call Sp(4, ·). It is only a naming convention. Safe to skip on a first reading.
- Not new as a variety: Hunt says this threefold was already well studied, and that the ball side follows Holzapfel's work on the surface case closely. The new part is the comparison. Safe to skip.
- Γ(2) does not act freely: the quotient is smooth all the same, by results of Igusa that Hunt cites. Safe to skip.
- Rank: 𝕊<sub>2</sub> has real rank 2 and the ball has real rank 1, so the two structures are different in kind and not only in name. Safe to skip.
- Scan quality: the Numdam PDF is a scan, and some symbols in its text layer are broken. Read the page images, not copied text. Safe to skip.
