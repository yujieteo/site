## Before you start

This survey moves fast through four subjects: polytopes, loop spaces, posets and a new kind of algebra. The likely feeling is a scattered unease, a sense that you ought to know all four. You do not need to, and the unease is fine. Each section stands alone and starts from pictures of trees. If you want one object that links topology to algebra and that you can draw by hand, this is it. Enjoy the pentagon. The rest can come later, or never.

## The story

A product of n + 2 letters can be bracketed in many ways. In a loop space these bracketings give different loops, joined by homotopies, and those homotopies are joined by higher ones. Stasheff showed in 1966 that this data forms a cell decomposition of a ball, with one vertex per bracketing. For five bracketings of four letters it is a pentagon. People wanted this complex as an honest convex polytope. A published proof came only in 1989. Loday gives a short recipe: turn each bracketing into a planar binary tree, read off integer coordinates from the tree, and take the convex hull. The same polytope then yields a partial order on trees, a product on the span of trees, and the coefficients for inverting a power series.

## Main definitions

**Planar binary tree.** Y<sub>n</sub> is the set of planar binary trees with n internal vertices, hence n + 1 leaves. They correspond to the bracketings of a word with n + 1 letters. Grafting t ∨ s joins the roots of t and s to a new root, and every tree with n > 0 is t<sub>l</sub> ∨ t<sub>r</sub> in exactly one way.

**The point M(t).** Number the leaves 0 to n and the internal vertices 1 to n, so that vertex i sits between leaves i − 1 and i. Let a<sub>i</sub> and b<sub>i</sub> be the numbers of leaves to the left and right below vertex i. Then M(t) = (a<sub>1</sub>b<sub>1</sub>, …, a<sub>n</sub>b<sub>n</sub>) ∈ ℝ<sup>n</sup>.
- Example: the bracketing (((x<sub>0</sub>x<sub>1</sub>)x<sub>2</sub>)(x<sub>3</sub>x<sub>4</sub>)) gives (1, 2, 6, 1).

**Tamari order.** The partial order on Y<sub>n</sub> generated by one local move: replace a left-leaning fork by the right-leaning one. In bracket terms, (ab)c becomes a(bc).

**Dendriform algebra.** A vector space with two operations ≺ and ≻ such that (x ≺ y) ≺ z = x ≺ (y \* z), (x ≻ y) ≺ z = x ≻ (y ≺ z) and (x \* y) ≻ z = x ≻ (y ≻ z), where x \* y = x ≺ y + x ≻ y. The product \* is then associative.

## Main results

**Theorem (Loday's realisation; Lemma 2.1 and Definition 2.2).** Every point M(t) with t ∈ Y<sub>n</sub> lies in the hyperplane x<sub>1</sub> + … + x<sub>n</sub> = n(n + 1)/2. The convex hull of these points is a polytope of dimension n − 1, the associahedron K<sup>n−1</sup>. As a cell complex it is the Stasheff complex.
- Step 1: for t = t<sub>l</sub> ∨ t<sub>r</sub>, the point M(t) is M(t<sub>l</sub>), then (p + 1)(q + 1), then M(t<sub>r</sub>). Induction gives the hyperplane.
- Step 2: each face lies in a hyperplane where k consecutive coordinates sum to k(k + 1)/2, and it is a product of two smaller associahedra.

**Proposition (from the permutohedron; Proposition 4.2).** Send a permutation to the tree obtained by forgetting levels in a levelled tree. Let C be the centre of the permutohedron. Then the vector from C to M(t) is the sum of the vectors from C to M(σ) over all permutations σ that map to t.

**Theorem (product as an interval; Theorem 5.2, due to Loday and Ronco).** Define t \* s = t<sub>l</sub> ∨ (t<sub>r</sub> \* s) + (t \* s<sub>l</sub>) ∨ s<sub>r</sub> on the span of all trees, with the one-leaf tree as unit. Then t \* s is the sum of all trees x with t/s ≤ x ≤ t\s in the Tamari order.
- Step 1: t/s puts the root of t on the leftmost leaf of s, and t\s puts the root of s on the rightmost leaf of t.
- Step 2: the two summands of the product define ≺ and ≻, and the span of trees is the free dendriform algebra on one generator.

**Theorem (series inversion; Section 6).** Let f(x) = x + a<sub>1</sub>x<sup>2</sup> + a<sub>2</sub>x<sup>3</sup> + … and let g be its inverse under composition. Up to sign, the coefficient of a<sub>1</sub><sup>n<sub>1</sub></sup>⋯a<sub>k</sub><sup>n<sub>k</sub></sup> in b<sub>n</sub> is the number of cells of K<sup>n−1</sup> isomorphic to (K<sup>0</sup>)<sup>n<sub>1</sub></sup> × … × (K<sup>k−1</sup>)<sup>n<sub>k</sub></sup>.

## Note, then ignore

- Indexing. Loday writes K<sup>n</sup> for the polytope of dimension n and notes that others write K<sub>n+2</sub> or K<sub>n+1</sub>. This is only a labelling choice. Safe to skip on a first reading.
- Poles. Loday calls the fully left-bracketed vertex the North pole and the fully right-bracketed one the South pole, so that the Tamari order runs down the edges. It is a way to picture the order. Safe to skip.
- Zinbiel algebras. A commutative dendriform algebra reduces to one operation with one relation. It is a side branch. Safe to skip.
- Proofs. Most statements are quoted from Loday's other papers, and one proof reads only "By induction". The survey aims at the picture. Safe to skip.
- Section 7. It lists open questions, a trefoil knot drawn on K<sup>3</sup> and a Hopf algebra structure. None is needed for the main line. Safe to skip.
