## Before you start

These notes run to about 110 pages, lean on a previous course on condensed mathematics, and hold a proof that even the author found hard to keep in his head. Overwhelm is a fair response, and you can let it be there. Nothing asks you to verify the proof. If you want to see analysis done with the tools of algebraic geometry, Lecture I alone gives you the plan. Read for the pleasure of the idea. How far you get comes second.

## The story

Algebraic geometry starts from abelian groups, rings and modules, then glues. To do the same for analytic geometry you need an abelian category of "topological" abelian groups with a good tensor product. Topological abelian groups fail: the identity map from ℝ with the discrete topology to ℝ with the usual topology has no kernel and no cokernel, yet is not an isomorphism. Condensed abelian groups fix that. A second problem remains. You must say which infinite sums converge. Over ℚ<sub>p</sub> the "solid" theory does this, but ℝ is not solid. The notes build a replacement over ℝ. Convex (ℓ<sup>1</sup>) completeness breaks under extensions, so they weaken it to ℓ<sup>p</sup> conditions for all p′ &lt; p. The result is the category of p-liquid vector spaces. From such analytic rings the last lectures glue analytic spaces.

## Main definitions

**Condensed set (Definition 1.1).** A sheaf on the category of profinite sets, with covers given by finite families of jointly surjective maps. A topological space T gives the condensed set S ↦ Cont(S, T). Condensed abelian groups form an abelian category.

**Solid abelian group (Definition 2.4).** A condensed abelian group M such that every map S → M from a profinite set S = lim S<sub>i</sub> extends uniquely to a map from ℤ[S]<sup>■</sup> = lim ℤ[S<sub>i</sub>] to M.
- Works for: ℤ and ℚ<sub>p</sub>, where the free solid ring on a null sequence is the power series ring ℤ[[T]].
- Fails for: ℝ, since Σ r<sub>n</sub>(1/2)<sup>n</sup> does not converge for all integers r<sub>n</sub>.

**The spaces M<sub>p</sub>(S) (Definition 6.3).** For 0 &lt; p ≤ 1 and a finite set S, let ℝ[S]<sub>ℓ<sup>p</sup>≤c</sub> be the vectors (a<sub>s</sub>) with Σ |a<sub>s</sub>|<sup>p</sup> ≤ c. For profinite S = lim S<sub>i</sub>, M<sub>p</sub>(S) is the union over c > 0 of the limits over i of these sets. Then M<sub>&lt;p</sub>(S) is the union of M<sub>p′</sub>(S) over p′ &lt; p.

**p-liquid vector space (Theorem 6.5).** A condensed ℝ-vector space V such that every map S → V from a profinite set extends uniquely to an ℝ-linear map M<sub>&lt;p</sub>(S) → V.
- Equivalent: unique extension to M<sub>p′</sub>(S) for every p′ &lt; p.
- Equivalent: V is the cokernel of a map between direct sums of spaces M<sub>&lt;p</sub>(S).

## Main results

**Theorem (solid abelian groups; Theorem 2.5, recalled from the earlier course).** Solid abelian groups form a subcategory of condensed abelian groups closed under all limits, colimits and extensions. The inclusion has a left adjoint, solidification, and there is a unique tensor product that makes solidification symmetric monoidal.

**Theorem (liquid vector spaces; Theorem 6.5).** Fix 0 &lt; p ≤ 1. The p-liquid ℝ-vector spaces form an abelian subcategory of condensed ℝ-vector spaces, stable under kernels, cokernels and extensions, with compact projective generators M<sub>&lt;p</sub>(S) for S extremally disconnected. The inclusion has a left adjoint, and a unique tensor product makes it symmetric monoidal. The same holds on derived categories.
- Step 1: reduce to a statement about RHom out of M<sub>p′</sub>(S) (Theorem 6.6).
- Step 2: move from ℝ to an arithmetic ring of Laurent series, where the pieces are profinite (Theorems 6.9 to 6.11, then 9.1).

**Theorem (ℝ as a quotient of Laurent series; Theorem 6.9).** Let 0 &lt; r′ &lt; r &lt; 1 and let ℤ((T))<sub>r</sub> be the Laurent series Σ a<sub>n</sub>T<sup>n</sup> with integer coefficients and Σ |a<sub>n</sub>|r<sup>n</sup> &lt; ∞. Evaluation at T = r′ is a surjection onto ℝ. Its kernel is generated by one nonzerodivisor. The matching module of measures on S, modulo that element, is M<sub>p</sub>(S), where (r′)<sup>p</sup> = r.

**Definition and claim (analytic spaces; Definitions 13.5 and 13.7).** An analytic space is a sheaf on analytic rings covered by affine pieces AnSpec(A, M) along "steady" subspaces. Each one carries an ∞-category of quasicoherent sheaves, defined as a limit over its affine pieces. The preface states that adic spaces and complex-analytic spaces are examples. I did not check that statement in the later lectures.

## Note, then ignore

- Set-theoretic size. The category of all profinite sets is large, so the definition of condensed sets needs a cutoff. The notes say they mostly ignore this. Skip it.
- Quasiseparated. This is the condensed version of Hausdorff. It appears in M-completeness, the failed first attempt in Lecture IV. Skip it on a first reading.
- The choice of p. There is a different liquid theory for each p, so "the" real numbers come in a one-parameter family. The story is the same for every p. Skip the comparison.
- B<sub>dR</sub><sup>+</sup> for the reals. Lecture V and the remarks after Definition 6.3 build a real analogue of a p-adic period ring. The notes say they have no direct use for it. Skip it.
- Base ring ℤ[T<sup>−1</sup>]. The proof needs the finer statement over ℤ[T<sup>−1</sup>], not just over the Laurent series ring. This is a proof device. Skip it.
