## Before you start

This is a 78-page handbook chapter, and its first line warns that it is full of conjectures. The likely feeling is overwhelm: too many names, too many arrows between them, and a fear of not knowing which statements are proved. That feeling is fair. The chapter is a map, not a proof to check. If you want to see why K-groups should know about zeta values, this is the map. Wander in it for pleasure. Finishing is optional.

## The story

In topology, complex K-theory and singular cohomology are tied by the Atiyah–Hirzebruch spectral sequence and the Chern character. Algebraic geometers wanted the same picture. Algebraic K-theory, built from vector bundles, came first and is easier to handle. The missing partner was a cohomology built from algebraic cycles. Bloch's higher Chow groups, and later Suslin–Voevodsky's motivic cohomology, fill that place, and a spectral sequence now runs from motivic cohomology to K-theory. Kahn uses this picture to organise the conjectures of Soulé and Lichtenbaum, which read orders of zeros and special values of zeta functions from these groups. He shows which conjectures imply which. Then, for varieties over finite fields built from abelian varieties, he proves some of them outright.

## Main definitions

**Higher Chow groups.** For X quasi-projective over a field k, let z<sub>n</sub>(X, p) be the group of cycles of dimension n + p on X × Δ<sup>p</sup> that meet all faces properly. Here Δ<sup>p</sup> is the algebraic p-simplex. The homology of this complex is CH<sub>n</sub>(X, p).
- For p = 0: this is the classical Chow group CH<sub>n</sub>(X).
- For X smooth of dimension d: write CH<sup>n</sup>(X, p) = CH<sub>d−n</sub>(X, p), and motivic cohomology is H<sup>p</sup>(X, ℤ(n)) = CH<sup>n</sup>(X, 2n − p).

**Zeta function of a scheme.** For X of finite type over ℤ, ζ(X, s) is the product over closed points x of (1 − N(x)<sup>−s</sup>)<sup>−1</sup>, where N(x) is the size of the residue field (Section 4.7.3).

**Tate and Beilinson conjectures.** Let X be smooth and projective over a finite field. Let A<sup>n</sup><sub>rat</sub>(X, ℚ) and A<sup>n</sup><sub>num</sub>(X, ℚ) be codimension n cycles with ℚ coefficients, modulo rational and numerical equivalence.
- Tate (Conjecture 49): the order of ζ(X, s) at s = n equals −dim A<sup>n</sup><sub>num</sub>(X, ℚ).
- Beilinson (Conjecture 50): A<sup>n</sup><sub>rat</sub>(X, ℚ) = A<sup>n</sup><sub>num</sub>(X, ℚ).

**Varieties of abelian type.** B(k) is the class of smooth projective X over k whose Chow motive lies in the thick rigid subcategory generated by Artin motives and motives of abelian varieties. B<sub>tate</sub>(k) is the subclass for which the Tate conjecture holds on ℓ-adic cohomology for some ℓ ≠ char k (Definition 74).

## Main results

**Theorem (Nesterenko–Suslin, Totaro; Theorem 2).** For a field k, CH<sub>−n</sub>(k, n) ≅ K<sup>M</sup><sub>n</sub>(k), the Milnor K-group. So Milnor's symbols are a piece of motivic cohomology, not an ad hoc construction.

**Theorem (motivic Atiyah–Hirzebruch spectral sequence; display 4.6).** For X smooth over a field there is a spectral sequence with E<sub>2</sub><sup>p,q</sup> = H<sup>p</sup>(X, ℤ(−q/2)), taken as 0 for q odd, converging to K<sub>−p−q</sub>(X). It is due to Bloch–Lichtenbaum, Friedlander–Suslin and Levine.
- Consequence: up to groups of finite exponent, the weight j part of K<sub>i</sub>(X) under Adams operations is a single motivic cohomology group.

**Theorem (implications among conjectures; Theorems 52 and 58).** For smooth projective varieties over a finite field, Tate plus Beilinson imply the Beilinson–Parshin conjecture that K<sub>i</sub>(X) is torsion for i > 0 (Geisser). The combined Tate–Beilinson conjecture implies Soulé's conjecture, which gives the order of ζ(X, s) at s = n as an alternating sum of dimensions of weight n parts of K′-groups.

**Theorem (abelian type; Theorem 76).** Let k be a finite field. The Tate conjecture and the Beilinson conjecture hold for every X in B<sub>tate</sub>(k). Examples include products of elliptic curves.
- Step 1: Tate's conjecture follows from semisimplicity of Frobenius on abelian varieties.
- Step 2: Beilinson's conjecture follows from Kimura–O'Sullivan finite dimensionality of these Chow motives, which makes the kernel from rational to numerical equivalence nilpotent.

## Note, then ignore

- K versus K′. K comes from vector bundles and K′ from coherent sheaves. They agree for regular schemes, so the difference only matters for singular X. Safe to skip on a first reading.
- Quillen versus Thomason–Trobaugh K-theory. The second fixes a functoriality gap and agrees with the first for quasi-projective or regular schemes. Safe to skip.
- Status of Bloch–Kato. The chapter dates from 2005 and treats the Bloch–Kato conjecture as proved for ℓ = 2 and not yet fully written for odd ℓ. Read conditional statements with that date in mind, then move on.
- Renumbering of higher Chow groups. The chapter switches between CH<sub>n</sub>(X, p), Borel–Moore motivic homology and H<sup>p</sup>(X, ℤ(n)). It is one family of groups under three indexings. Safe to skip.
- Lichtenbaum's Weil-étale topology. It refines the special-value conjectures to integral statements. The story above only needs orders of zeros. Safe to skip.
