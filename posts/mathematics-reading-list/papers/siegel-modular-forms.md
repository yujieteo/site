## Before you start

These lecture notes are 56 pages in 27 sections, and they pass through compactifications, the Satake isomorphism and étale cohomology. The likely feeling is the worry of being under-prepared: each section leans on a subject you may know only by name. That worry is an honest response to the range, and you do not need to get rid of it. If you want to see how elliptic modular forms generalise, and why the generalisation has to be vector-valued, the first few sections say it with matrices. Read them for interest. Nobody is counting sections.

## The story

A modular form on SL(2, ℤ) lives on the upper half-plane. Siegel replaced the group by Sp(2g, ℤ) and the half-plane by H<sub>g</sub>, the symmetric g × g complex matrices with positive definite imaginary part. Van der Geer argues that these forms matter in number theory and algebraic geometry but have a poor reputation, mainly for lack of examples you can compute with. For g > 1, Fourier coefficients do not hand you Hecke eigenvalues. His way round uses geometry. The quotient Sp(2g, ℤ)\H<sub>g</sub> is the moduli space A<sub>g</sub> of principally polarised abelian varieties. For g = 2 these are Jacobians of genus 2 curves or products of elliptic curves. Cusp forms sit inside the cohomology of local systems on A<sub>2</sub>. So, in joint work with Faber, he lists genus 2 curves over 𝔽<sub>q</sub>, counts points, and reads off traces of Hecke operators. The numbers then support a congruence conjectured by Harder.

## Main definitions

**Siegel modular group and half-space.** Γ<sub>g</sub> = Sp(2g, ℤ) is the automorphism group of ℤ<sup>2g</sup> with its standard symplectic form. It acts on H<sub>g</sub> by τ ↦ (aτ + b)(cτ + d)<sup>−1</sup>, where a, b, c, d are the g × g blocks of the matrix.
- g = 1: this is SL(2, ℤ) acting on the upper half-plane.

**Siegel modular form of weight ρ.** Let ρ: GL(g, ℂ) → GL(V) be a finite-dimensional representation. A form of weight ρ is a holomorphic f: H<sub>g</sub> → V with f(γ(τ)) = ρ(cτ + d) f(τ) for all γ ∈ Γ<sub>g</sub>. For g = 1 one also asks for holomorphy at ∞.
- Classical case: ρ = det<sup>k</sup>, giving M<sub>k</sub>(Γ<sub>g</sub>).
- Genus 2: ρ = Sym<sup>j</sup> ⊗ det<sup>k</sup>, giving M<sub>j,k</sub>(Γ<sub>2</sub>) and its cusp forms S<sub>j,k</sub>(Γ<sub>2</sub>). These vanish for odd j.

**Local systems on A<sub>2</sub>.** Let V be the local system whose fibre at an abelian surface X is H<sup>1</sup>(X, ℚ). For l ≥ m ≥ 0, V<sub>l,m</sub> is the local system built from the irreducible representation of Sp(4, ℚ) with those indices. It is called regular if l > m > 0.

## Main results

**Theorem (Koecher principle; Theorem 4.4).** Write f ∈ M<sub>ρ</sub>(Γ<sub>g</sub>) as a Fourier series Σ a(n) q<sup>n</sup> over half-integral symmetric matrices n. Then a(n) = 0 unless n is positive semi-definite. The notes use this to explain why no condition at infinity is needed for g > 1. One consequence (Corollary 4.5) is that a classical form of negative weight is zero.

**Theorem (Igusa; Theorem 9.1).** The graded ring of classical Siegel modular forms of genus 2 is generated by E<sub>4</sub>, E<sub>6</sub>, χ<sub>10</sub>, χ<sub>12</sub> and χ<sub>35</sub>. The only relation expresses χ<sub>35</sub><sup>2</sup> as an explicit polynomial in the other four, each of whose monomials has weight 70.
- Consequence: the forms of even weight make a polynomial ring in E<sub>4</sub>, E<sub>6</sub>, χ<sub>10</sub>, χ<sub>12</sub>.

**Theorem (cusp forms in cohomology; Section 24, credited to Faltings).** For regular (l, m), the interior cohomology H<sup>i</sup><sub>!</sub>(A<sub>2</sub>, V<sub>l,m</sub>) is zero for i ≠ 3. In degree 3 it carries a Hodge filtration whose smallest step is isomorphic to S<sub>l−m, m+3</sub>(Γ<sub>2</sub>).
- Case g = 1: this is the analogue of the Eichler-Shimura theorem (Theorem 23.1), which finds S<sub>k+2</sub>(Γ<sub>1</sub>) and its conjugate in the cohomology of Sym<sup>k</sup> of the basic local system.
- New in g = 2: the middle steps of the filtration can hide an "endoscopic" part. Its size is Conjecture 24.2 in the notes, not a theorem there.

**Conjecture (Harder; Conjecture 26.2).** Let f ∈ S<sub>r</sub>(Γ<sub>1</sub>) be a normalised eigenform with eigenvalues a(p). Suppose a "large" prime ℓ divides the critical value Λ(f, t). Then there is a Hecke eigenform F ∈ S<sub>j,k</sub>(Γ<sub>2</sub>) with j = 2t − r − 2 and k = r − t + 2 whose eigenvalues satisfy λ(p) ≡ p<sup>k−2</sup> + a(p) + p<sup>j+k−1</sup> modulo a prime above ℓ, for all primes p.
- Evidence (Proposition 27.1): for the weight 22 cusp form and ℓ = 41, the space S<sub>4,10</sub>(Γ<sub>2</sub>) has dimension 1, and λ(p) ≡ p<sup>8</sup> + a(p) + p<sup>13</sup> mod 41 holds for all p ≤ 37.
- Caveat: the eigenvalues λ(p) come from the curve counts and assume the endoscopic conjecture.

## Note, then ignore

- Notation for blocks. The author complains about capital letters A, B, C, D for the blocks and switches to lower case. Both appear in the notes. Safe to skip on a first reading.
- Level one only. The notes treat the full group Sp(2g, ℤ) and leave congruence subgroups aside. Safe to skip.
- Orbifold, not manifold. Sp(2g, ℤ) does not act freely on H<sub>g</sub>. The level n subgroups do for n ≥ 3, which is why they appear briefly in Section 2. Safe to skip.
- Compactifications. The Satake compactification is singular and the toroidal ones are smooth. Sections 11 and 22 need them to make cohomology behave. Safe to skip.
- Galois representations. The author says outright that the Galois representations attached to Siegel modular forms are left out. Safe to skip.
