## Before you start

This is a 330-page volume by many authors, half of it computer sessions. The likely feeling is overwhelm, mixed with a doubt that typing commands counts as mathematics. Both feelings are fine to have. Let them sit. You may want to see a scheme as something you can poke, the way you would poke a mechanism on a bench. One chapter is enough. Play with one example first. Coverage can wait.

## The story

Algebraic geometry studies solution sets of polynomial systems. Its invariants (dimension, degree, free resolutions, sheaf cohomology) have clean definitions, but hand computation stops at toy cases. Gröbner bases promised a general method, yet worst-case estimates suggested the method was hopeless. Then Bayer and Stillman wrote Macaulay in 1983 to 1993, and it worked far more often than the estimates predicted, perhaps because problems from geometry are special. Macaulay 2, by Grayson and Stillman, is a fresh start with a real programming language. The book teaches by example. Part I introduces the system through curves, the twisted cubic and schemes. Part II gives seven research topics, each with its algorithms. The first session takes the ideal (x<sup>4</sup> − y<sup>5</sup>, x<sup>3</sup> − y<sup>7</sup>), finds degree 28, strips off a multiple line, and leaves 13 straight lines.

## Main definitions

**Ring first.** In Macaulay 2 you must create a ring before you write a polynomial. Rings, ideals, modules, maps, chain complexes, varieties and sheaves are all objects you can name and pass to functions.
- Example: R = QQ[x,y,z]/(x^3-y^3-z^3) makes a quotient ring, and Proj R makes a projective variety.

**Initial ideal and standard monomials.** Fix a term order. The initial ideal in(I) is generated by the leading monomials of all elements of I, and a Gröbner basis is a generating set whose leading terms generate in(I). The standard monomials are those outside in(I), the "monomials under the staircase". Dimension and degree are read from them.

**Borel-fixed ideal.** A monomial ideal I in S = ℚ[x<sub>0</sub>, …, x<sub>n</sub>] is Borel-fixed if g·I = I for every upper triangular matrix g in GL<sub>n+1</sub>(ℚ), acting by linear change of variables.

**Trace form.** Let I be a zero-dimensional ideal in k[x<sub>1</sub>, …, x<sub>n</sub>] and A = k[x]/I, a finite-dimensional k-algebra. For a polynomial h, the trace form is S<sub>h</sub>(f, g) = trace of multiplication by hfg on A. It is a symmetric bilinear form on A.

## Main results

**Theorem (Galligo, generic initial ideal; Theorem 4.1 of "Monomial Ideals").** Fix a term order on S = ℚ[x<sub>0</sub>, …, x<sub>n</sub>] with x<sub>0</sub> > … > x<sub>n</sub>, and let I be a homogeneous ideal. Then there is a Zariski open subset U of GL<sub>n+1</sub>(ℚ) and a monomial ideal J with in(g·I) = J for all g in U. The ideal J is Borel-fixed.
- The book gives no proof and cites Green. It computes J by applying a random change of variables.

**Theorem (Bézout; Theorem 1.1 of Sottile's chapter).** A system of n polynomial equations in n variables, of degrees d<sub>1</sub>, …, d<sub>n</sub>, has at most d<sub>1</sub>d<sub>2</sub>⋯d<sub>n</sub> isolated solutions. Generic polynomials attain the bound over an algebraically closed field.

**Theorem (real roots from the trace form; Theorem 2.2 of Sottile's chapter).** Let k ⊂ ℝ and let I be zero-dimensional. Then the rank of S<sub>h</sub> is the number of points a of V(I) in ℂ<sup>n</sup> with h(a) ≠ 0. The signature of S<sub>h</sub> is the number of real points with h(a) > 0 minus the number with h(a) &lt; 0.
- Case h = 1: the signature counts the real solutions.

**Theorem (cohomology from the Tate resolution; Theorem 3.1 of Decker and Eisenbud's chapter).** Let M be a finitely generated graded module over the polynomial ring S, with associated sheaf F on projective space. The Tate resolution T(M) is a complex of free modules over the exterior algebra E. Its term in cohomological degree i is the direct sum over j of H<sup>j</sup>(F(i − j)) ⊗ ω<sub>E</sub>, where ω<sub>E</sub> = Hom<sub>K</sub>(E, K).
- Step 1: the linear complex R(M) is exact in degrees above the Castelnuovo-Mumford regularity of M (Theorem 2.1 there).
- Step 2: extend backwards by a free resolution over E. Every cohomology group of every twist then appears exactly once.

## Note, then ignore

- Software age. The preface dates from May 2001, and it mentions Emacs, MPW and an old download address. Install a current Macaulay 2 instead. Safe to skip the installation paragraphs.
- Output layout. Exponents print on the line above their bases, and long lines end in dots. This is how the program prints. Safe to ignore.
- Default order. The default monomial order is graded reverse lexicographic. It matters only when you compare your Gröbner basis with the printed one. Safe to skip at first.
- Rational coefficients. Galligo's theorem is stated over ℚ, and a random matrix lies in U with probability one in characteristic zero. Safe to skip on a first reading.
- Chapter independence. Each chapter has its own abstract, notation and bibliography. You lose nothing by reading out of order.
