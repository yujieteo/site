### Ordering by divisibility makes gcd(0,0) equal 0

**Remember:** Everything divides 0, so 0 sits at the top of the divisibility order.

*Makes the theorem clean to state or prove.* MSC 11A05 (Multiplicative structure; Euclidean algorithm; greatest common divisors), 06B23. arXiv math.NT, math.CO. Level 1: early undergraduate.

**Definition.** The greatest common divisor of a and b is the common divisor that every other common divisor divides. Here "greatest" means greatest in the divisibility order, where x comes below y when x divides y. In this order, 0 is the greatest element, because every integer divides 0. So gcd(0,0) = 0.

**Problem.** The theorems are Bézout's identity, which says that gcd(a,b) generates the same ideal as a and b, and the statement that the natural numbers form a complete distributive lattice under gcd and lcm. (A lattice is an order in which every pair has a greatest lower bound and a least upper bound. It is complete when every subset has them.)

**How the definition helps.** Measuring "greatest" by divisibility, not by size, makes gcd(0,0) exist, so the theorems hold for all pairs. If "greatest" means largest in size, gcd(0,0) is undefined, because every integer divides 0. With the divisibility order, gcd(0,0) = 0. Then Bézout's identity holds for all pairs: the ideal generated by 0 and 0 is the ideal (0). The article adds that with gcd(0,0) = lcm(0,0) = 0, the natural numbers form a complete lattice. If 0 is left out, this fails.

**Wikipedia:** [Greatest common divisor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greatest_common_divisor). It says: “However, zero is its own greatest divisor if greatest is understood in the context of the divisibility relation, so gcd(0, 0) is commonly defined as 0. This preserves the usual identities for GCD, and in particular Bézout's identity”

### Divisible differences make congruence respect sums and products

**Remember:** The differences of the sums and products are still multiples of m.

*True by definition. Confidence: medium.* MSC 11A07 (Congruences; primitive roots; residue systems). arXiv math.NT. Level 1: early undergraduate.

**Definition.** For integers a and b and a positive integer m, a ≡ b modulo m means that m divides a − b. Equivalently, a and b have the same remainder on division by m.

**Problem.** If a ≡ b and c ≡ d modulo m, then a + c ≡ b + d and ac ≡ bd modulo m.

**How the definition helps.** The divisibility definition turns compatibility with arithmetic into closure of multiples of m under addition and multiplication by integers. For sums, (a + c) − (b + d) = (a − b) + (c − d). For products, ac − bd = a(c − d) + d(a − b). So both differences are multiples of m (our own). With the remainder definition, one instead substitutes quotient-and-remainder expressions for the inputs and compares the output remainders. The division algorithm supplies the equivalence of the definitions.

### The Jacobi symbol gives quadratic reciprocity without factoring

**Remember:** Jacobi multiplies the Legendre symbols over the prime factors of the bottom number.

*Makes the theorem clean to state or prove.* MSC 11A15 (Power residues, reciprocity). arXiv math.NT. Level 1: early undergraduate.

**Definition.** Let a be any integer, and let n = p₁⋯pₖ be an odd positive integer, where the primes need not be different. The Jacobi symbol (a/n) is the product of the Legendre symbols (a/p₁)⋯(a/pₖ). The Legendre symbol (a/p) is 0 when the prime p divides a. Otherwise it is 1 when a is a square modulo p, and −1 when a is not.

**Problem.** Quadratic reciprocity holds for all odd, coprime, positive m and n. This gives a fast algorithm for (a/p), similar to the Euclidean algorithm.

**How the definition helps.** The Jacobi symbol lets you flip the symbol without first factoring the top number. With only the Legendre symbol, you can flip (a/p) to (p/a) only when a is prime, so you must factor a first (our own remark). The Jacobi symbol extends the symbol to odd composite bottom numbers. Its reciprocity law follows directly from the definition and the Legendre case. So the article's algorithm computes the symbol in O(log a log b) steps without factoring, like the Euclidean algorithm. The price is a loss of meaning: (a/n) = 1 no longer means that a is a square modulo n.

**Wikipedia:** [Jacobi symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobi_symbol). It says: “The following facts, even the reciprocity laws, are straightforward deductions from the definition of the Jacobi symbol and the corresponding properties of the Legendre symbol.”

### The Legendre symbol turns quadratic reciprocity into one formula

**Remember:** (a/p) is a sign that says whether a is a square mod p.

*Makes the theorem clean to state or prove. Confidence: medium.* MSC 11A15 (Power residues, reciprocity). arXiv math.NT. Level 1: early undergraduate.

**Definition.** Let p be an odd prime. The Legendre symbol (a/p) is 1 if a is a nonzero square modulo p. It is −1 if a is not a square modulo p, and 0 if p divides a.

**Problem.** Quadratic reciprocity says this: for distinct odd primes p and q, (p/q)·(q/p) = (−1) to the power ((p−1)/2)·((q−1)/2). In words, the product is −1 exactly when p and q both leave remainder 3 on division by 4.

**How the definition helps.** In our own reading, the symbol helps because it is a number, ±1, so you can multiply it, and the law becomes one formula. Gauss wrote aRp and aNp for the same fact (a is a residue, or a non-residue, of p). Also in our own reading, Gauss's notation is a statement and not a number, so you cannot multiply it. With it, the law is a list of cases by the remainders of p and q on division by 4. Legendre introduced his symbol while he tried to prove reciprocity. The symbol is completely multiplicative in a, so it is a Dirichlet character (a completely multiplicative function of a that depends only on a modulo a fixed number). Proving the law is still the hard part.

### Treating 0 separately gives equal numbers of quadratic residues and nonresidues

**Remember:** Leave zero out, and residues are exactly half of the nonzero classes.

*Rules out counterexamples.* MSC 11A15 (Power residues, reciprocity). arXiv math.NT. Level 1: early undergraduate.

**Definition.** An integer a is a quadratic residue modulo n when a ≡ x² mod n for some x. Modulo an odd prime p, the usual convention leaves 0 out, so residues and nonresidues are nonzero classes.

**Problem.** Modulo an odd prime p, there are (p − 1)/2 residues and (p − 1)/2 nonresidues. The product of two nonresidues is a residue, and a residue times a nonresidue is a nonresidue.

**How the definition helps.** Without 0, the residues are the squares in the cyclic group of nonzero classes (our own). So they form a subgroup of index 2 (our own). Then all the product rules hold at once, and the Legendre symbol is a group homomorphism to ±1 (our own). With 0 counted as a residue, there are (p + 1)/2 residues against (p − 1)/2 nonresidues. The rule that a residue times a nonresidue is a nonresidue also fails, since 0 times anything is 0 (our own). For composite moduli the nonzero classes do not form a group, and the rules can fail. Quadratic reciprocity, the hard theorem, is not touched by this convention (our own).

### Requiring f(1) = 1 makes multiplicative functions a group

**Remember:** Asking f(1) = 1 throws out only the zero function, which has no inverse.

*Rules out counterexamples.* MSC 11A25 (Arithmetic functions; related numbers; inversion formulas). arXiv math.NT. Level 1: early undergraduate.

**Definition.** An arithmetic function f is multiplicative when f(1) = 1 and f(mn) = f(m)f(n) for all coprime positive integers m and n. The Dirichlet convolution of f and g is (f∗g)(n) = Σ f(d) g(n/d), summed over the divisors d of n.

**Problem.** Under Dirichlet convolution, the multiplicative functions form an abelian group. Its identity is ε, with ε(1) = 1 and ε(n) = 0 for n &gt; 1. For example, the Möbius function μ is the inverse of the constant function 1.

**How the definition helps.** The condition f(1) = 1 removes exactly the one function that has no inverse. The product rule for coprime m and n alone gives f(1) = f(1)², so f(1) is 0 or 1 (our own). If f(1) = 0, then f(n) = f(n)f(1) = 0 for every n (our own). A function has a Dirichlet inverse exactly when f(1) ≠ 0, so the zero function cannot be in a group. With f(1) = 1, the inverse can be built step by step from g(1) = 1, and it is again multiplicative. This group structure makes Möbius inversion a one-line argument (our own).

It says: “every not constantly zero multiplicative function has a Dirichlet inverse which is also multiplicative. In other words, multiplicative functions form a subgroup of the group of invertible elements of the Dirichlet ring.”

### Leaving 1 out of the primes makes factorization unique

**Remember:** 1 is the empty product, with no factors to equal, so it is not prime.

*Rules out counterexamples.* MSC 11A41 (Primes), 11A05. arXiv math.NT. Level 1: early undergraduate.

The case k = 0 excludes 1, because 1 is the empty product and has no factors to be equal to.

**Problem.** The fundamental theorem of arithmetic says that every positive whole number is a product of primes in exactly one way, up to the order of the factors.

**How the definition helps.** Leaving 1 out lets the uniqueness statement hold with no exception. The unbiased definition excludes 1 automatically. The nLab calls this a case of a general principle: a trivial object is "too simple to be simple". The definition only makes the theorem statable; the proof of uniqueness (Euclid's lemma) is unchanged. The example with factors of 1 is our own illustration.

### Requiring the last partial quotient above 1 makes continued fractions unique

**Remember:** Forbid a final 1, and every rational has exactly one continued fraction.

*Rules out counterexamples.* MSC 11A55 (Continued fractions). arXiv math.NT. Level 1: early undergraduate.

**Definition.** A simple continued fraction \[a₀; a₁, …, aₙ\] is a₀ + 1/(a₁ + 1/(… + 1/aₙ)). Here a₀ is an integer and the other terms are positive integers. The canonical form also requires aₙ &gt; 1 when n ≥ 1.

**Problem.** Every rational number has exactly one canonical simple continued fraction. Its terms are the quotients of the Euclidean algorithm.

**How the definition helps.** The condition throws out the one way a finite continued fraction can be rewritten. Without it, every rational has two expansions, since \[a₀; …, aₙ\] = \[a₀; …, aₙ − 1, 1\]. For example, 3/2 = \[1; 2\] = \[1; 1, 1\] (our own). The condition aₙ &gt; 1 removes the longer one, and the Euclidean algorithm always gives the shorter one. For irrational numbers the expansion is infinite and unique with no extra condition. The theory of convergents and best approximation does not depend on this choice (our own).

### Defining e as the factorial series gives a short irrationality proof

**Remember:** Scaled by n!, the tail is a positive integer below one.

*True by definition.* MSC 11J72 (Irrationality; linear independence over a field), 26A09. arXiv math.CA. Level 1: early undergraduate.

**Definition.** Define e as the sum of 1/n! over all n ≥ 0. Another common definition is the limit of (1 + 1/n)ⁿ.

**Problem.** The number e is irrational.

**How the definition helps.** With the series definition, the tail after n terms is explicit and small, which drives the proof. Suppose e = p/q, where q ≥ 2 because 2 &lt; e &lt; 3 (our own). **How the definition helps.** The definition puts the whole law into one symmetric formula. The Hilbert symbol is defined at every place, including the real place and the prime 2. Then the law and its supplementary laws for −1 and 2 become one statement, ∏ᵥ (a,b)ᵥ = 1, a product over all places. In words, the conic has no point at an even number of places. The definition makes the law easy to state, not easy to prove: over ℚ the usual proof of the product formula uses quadratic reciprocity itself. The general version is Hilbert's reciprocity law, which follows from Artin reciprocity.

### Bounding modular forms at the cusp makes each space finite-dimensional

**Remember:** No poles at the cusp, so only finitely many forms fit.

**Problem.** For a subgroup G of finite index in SL₂(ℤ), the space Mₖ(G) of modular forms of weight k is finite-dimensional. For the full modular group, its dimension is about k/12, with an explicit formula.

**How the definition helps.** The growth condition forbids poles at the cusp, and a compact curve has only finitely many functions with bounded poles (our own). These functions are linearly independent, so the space would be infinite-dimensional (our own). With the condition, the forms live on the compact modular curve. There the Riemann–Roch theorem, a formula that counts functions with given poles, gives the dimension. The corrections from points with extra symmetry still need care (our own).

### Newforms give a Hecke eigenbasis including primes that divide the level

**Remember:** Throw out forms inherited from lower levels, and all Hecke operators diagonalize.

*Rules out counterexamples. Confidence: medium.* MSC 11F11 (Holomorphic modular forms of integral weight), 11F25. arXiv math.NT. Level 3: graduate.

**Definition.** The group Γ₀(N) consists of integer matrices of determinant 1 whose lower left entry is divisible by N, and its forms have level N. The oldforms of level N are spanned by the forms g(dz). Here g is a cusp form of a smaller level M dividing N, and d divides N/M. The newforms are the cusp forms orthogonal to all oldforms.

**Problem.** On the space of newforms of level N, all Hecke operators commute and are self-adjoint. This includes the operators Tₚ for primes p that divide N. So the newforms have a basis of eigenforms for the full Hecke algebra.

**How the definition helps.** Removing the oldforms removes the copies of lower-level forms, which the Hecke operators cannot separate. An oldform g(z) and its shift g(pz) have the same eigenvalues for the operators at primes not dividing N (our own). On the full cusp space, the operators at primes dividing N need not be self-adjoint (our own). The page says that on the newforms all Hecke operators are self-adjoint and commute. On the newforms, each system of eigenvalues occurs only once, which is the multiplicity-one theorem of Atkin and Lehner (our own). The Hecke operators here are averaging operators Tₙ on modular forms. The real work is Atkin and Lehner's study of the operators that move forms between levels (our own).

It says: “the algebra of operators on newforms they generate is a finite-dimensional C\*-algebra that is commutative; and by the spectral theory of such operators, there exists a basis for the space of newforms consisting of eigenforms for the full Hecke algebra.”

### Vanishing at the cusps makes the Petersson inner product finite

**Remember:** Forms that die at the cusp have finite length.

*Makes the theorem clean to state or prove.* MSC 11F11 (Holomorphic modular forms of integral weight), 11F25. arXiv math.NT. Level 3: graduate.

**Problem.** The Petersson integral of f ḡ yᵏ dx dy / y² over a fundamental domain converges when f or g is a cusp form. ### Normalizing eigenforms by a(1) = 1 makes coefficients equal Hecke eigenvalues

**Remember:** Set the first coefficient to one, and the coefficients become eigenvalues.

*True by definition.* MSC 11F25 (Hecke-Petersson operators, differential operators (one variable)), 11F30. arXiv math.NT. Level 3: graduate.

**Definition.** The Hecke operators Tₘ are natural averaging operators on modular forms of a fixed weight k. A Hecke eigenform is a nonzero cusp form f = Σ a(n) qⁿ with Tₘ f = λₘ f for every m. It is normalized when a(1) = 1.

**Problem.** For a normalized Hecke eigenform, Tₘ f = a(m) f for all m. The coefficients satisfy a(m)a(n) = Σ dᵏ⁻¹ a(mn/d²), summed over d dividing gcd(m,n). So the coefficients are multiplicative, and the L-function of f has an Euler product.

**How the definition helps.** Once a(1) = 1, the eigenvalue equation read at the first coefficient says a(m) = λₘ. The formula for Tₘ shows that the first coefficient of Tₘ f is a(m), so λₘ a(1) = a(m) (our own). Hence a(1) is not 0 for a nonzero eigenform, and one may scale it to 1 (our own). The multiplication rule of the Hecke operators then becomes a rule for the coefficients. For Ramanujan's Δ, the cusp form of weight 12, this gives τ(mn) = τ(m)τ(n) for coprime m and n. Mordell proved this in 1917, before Hecke's general theory. The work moves into showing that the operators commute and obey their multiplication rule.

### The Gauss map turns continued fraction digits into ergodic averages

### The narrow class group decides which primes a norm form represents

**Remember:** Signs matter for norm forms, so count ideals with positive generators.

*Makes the theorem clean to state or prove.* MSC 11R29 (Class numbers, class groups, discriminants), 11R11, 11E16. arXiv math.NT. Level 3: graduate.

**Definition.** An element of a number field is totally positive when it is positive under every embedding into the real numbers. The narrow class group is the group of fractional ideals modulo principal ideals with a totally positive generator. The ordinary class group allows any generator.

**Problem.** Let K = ℚ(√d) have trivial narrow class group and discriminant D, and let q be the norm form of an integral basis. A prime p equals q(x,y) for some integers exactly in three cases. Either p divides D, or p = 2 and D ≡ 1 mod 8, or p is odd and (D/p) = 1.

### Unramified at real places, the Hilbert class field matches the class group

**Problem.** The Galois group of the Hilbert class field over K is isomorphic to the ideal class group of K. Its degree is the class number.

**Remember:** Adeles look at a number field at all places at once, real and p-adic.

### Continued fractions with minus signs make the half-line a terminal coalgebra

**Remember:** Always round strictly up, so every number has only one expansion.

*Makes the theorem clean to state or prove.* MSC 11A55 (Continued fractions), 18C40. arXiv math.NT, math.CT. Level 4: research.

**Definition.** Write a real number x ≥ 1 as a continued fraction with minus signs: x = a₀ − 1/(a₁ − 1/(a₂ − ⋯)). At each step, let β(x) be the smallest integer strictly greater than x. Take β(x) as the next digit, and continue with the number −1/(x − β(x)). This gives a map ξ : ℝ≥1 → ℕ≥2 × ℝ≥1, which sends a number to its first digit and the rest.

**Problem.** We want the real numbers to be the terminal coalgebra of a simple functor. (A coalgebra of a functor F is a set X with a map X → F(X), and the terminal coalgebra is the universal one.) Then a real number is the same thing as its infinite stream of digits.

**How the definition helps.** Rounding strictly up removes the double expansions that stop ordinary continued fractions from working. With the usual continued fractions, the map from ℝ to its digit data is not an isomorphism. Rational numbers have two expansions, and the standard expansion of 2 is just 2, never 1 + 1/1, so no element maps to (1, 1, ∞). That is why ordinary continued fractions make only the irrationals a terminal coalgebra. Pavlović and Pratt use the lexicographic order on ℕ≥2 × X, and they prove that (ℝ≥1, ξ) is the terminal coalgebra on linear orders. In the other direction, the rationals greater than 1, together with ∞, form the initial algebra of the functor X ↦ (ℕ≥2 × X) + 1.

**Definition.** Let K₁ and K₂ be number fields with a common Galois closure L, and let Hᵢ = Gal(L∕Kᵢ) in G = Gal(L∕ℚ). The group G permutes the cosets of H₁ and of H₂, and this gives two permutation modules. The fields are locally integrally equivalent if these modules are isomorphic over the ℓ-adic integers for every prime ℓ.

**Problem.** Locally integrally equivalent number fields have isomorphic ideal class groups. The ideal class group measures how far ideals are from being principal. The fields also have the same zeta function and the same regulator.

**How the definition helps.** The definition keeps integer information at each prime ℓ, and this controls the ℓ-part of the class group. Equality of zeta functions only keeps rational information. In our own remark, fields with the same zeta function can have different class numbers, so the weaker definition does not give the theorem.

**Paper or page:** [Stronger arithmetic equivalence](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.01956v4). It says: “In addition to having the same Dedekind zeta function (the usual notion of arithmetic equivalence), number fields that are equivalent in any of these stronger senses must have the same class number”

**Sources:**

- [Andrew V. Sutherland, Stronger arithmetic equivalence, arXiv:2104.01956v4 (2021). Discrete Anal. 2021, Paper No. 23, 23 pp](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.01956v4)

### Normalized Haar measure gives Riemann–Roch from Poisson summation

**Remember:** Choose the measure's constant well, and Riemann–Roch falls out of Poisson summation.

*Makes the theorem clean to state or prove. Confidence: low.* MSC 11R56 (Adèle rings and groups), 43A05, 11R58. arXiv math.NT. Level 4: research.

**Definition.** A global field is a number field or the function field of a curve over a finite field. Its adeles are a locally compact ring that holds all its completions at once. A Haar measure on them is a translation-invariant measure, which is unique only up to a constant factor. The paper fixes that factor in a way that is relative to an extension of global fields.

**Problem.** Prove the relative Riemann–Roch theorem for an extension of global fields. For a curve, Riemann–Roch links the dimension of a space of functions with bounded poles to the degree and the genus (our own gloss). The paper covers number fields and curves over finite fields.

**How the definition helps.** Our own reason: with the right constant, the Poisson summation formula already has the shape of Riemann–Roch. Our own note: this follows Tate's thesis, where a self-dual Haar measure makes the volume terms match. With another constant, extra factors appear and must be tracked by hand (our own). The abstract says the paper also defines relative versions of the numbers h⁰ and h¹ from this setup.

**Paper or page:** [Harmonic analysis approach to the relative Riemann-Roch theorem on global fields](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.10424). It says: “In particular we show that using our relative normalization of the Haar measure on adeles we can obtain the relative Riemann-Roch theorem from the adelic Poisson summation formulae.”

**Sources:**

- [Weronika Czerniawska, Harmonic analysis approach to the relative Riemann-Roch theorem on global fields, arXiv:2208.10424 (2024)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.10424)

### Exceptional local field extensions give the ramification bound for exceptional covers

**Remember:** Complete at a rational point, then read off the degree.

*Makes the theorem clean to state or prove. Confidence: medium.* MSC 11S15 (Ramification and extension theory), 11G20. arXiv math.NT, math.AG. Level 4: research.

**Definition.** An exceptional cover of curves over 𝔽q is a bijection on points over the field with qᵏ elements, for infinitely many k. The paper defines a matching notion, an exceptional extension of local fields. For a totally ramified extension of degree n prime to q, Theorem 1.6 shows it is exceptional exactly when gcd(n, q − 1) = 1.

**Problem.** Let X → Y be an exceptional cover of curves over 𝔽q. A theorem of Guralnick and Müller says its ramification index at any 𝔽q-rational point of X is coprime to q − 1. This generalizes a result of Lenstra that settled conjectures of Carlitz and Wan.

**How the definition helps.** Completing at a rational point turns the global property into a local one, and exceptional local extensions have degree coprime to q − 1. Theorem 3.3 shows that the completed extension at an 𝔽q-rational point is exceptional. Theorem 2.1 shows that an exceptional local extension of degree n has gcd(n, q − 1) = 1. In our own words, at a rational point the residue field does not grow, so the local degree is the ramification index. The ramification index counts how many sheets of the cover come together at the point. The paper proves the needed local facts in three ways, with different tools.

**Paper or page:** [Exceptional extensions of local fields and the Carlitz--Wan conjecture](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.12877v1). It says: “As a consequence, we obtain three new proofs, using quite different tools, of a theorem of Guralnick and Müller about ramification indices in exceptional maps between curves over 𝔽q.”

**Sources:**

- [Zhiguo Ding, Wei Xiong, Qifan Zhang, Exceptional extensions of local fields and the Carlitz--Wan conjecture, arXiv:2505.12877v1 (2025)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.12877v1)
