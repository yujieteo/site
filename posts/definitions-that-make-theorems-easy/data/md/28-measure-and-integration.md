### Measure zero describes exactly the Riemann integrable functions

**Remember:** Countably many tiny intervals can hide a set of measure zero.

*Makes the theorem clean to state or prove.* MSC 28A12 (Contents, measures, outer measures, capacities), 26A42. arXiv math.CA. Level 1: early undergraduate.

**Definition.** A set of real numbers has measure zero if, for every ε &gt; 0, countably many intervals with total length less than ε cover it.

**Problem.** Lebesgue–Vitali theorem: a bounded function on \[a,b\] is Riemann integrable exactly when its points of discontinuity form a set of measure zero.

**How the definition helps.** With this definition, the answer fits in one short sentence and needs no full Lebesgue measure or integral. It shows at once that monotone functions are integrable, because they have only countably many discontinuities. Countable covers matter: the rationals in \[0,1\] have measure zero, but finitely many intervals of small total length cannot cover them (our own remark).

**Wikipedia:** [Riemann integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_integral). It says: “A bounded function on a compact interval \[a, b\] is Riemann integrable if and only if it is continuous almost everywhere (the set of its points of discontinuity has measure zero, in the sense of Lebesgue measure).”

### Dynkin systems make two measures that agree on intervals agree everywhere

**Remember:** Agreement sets form a Dynkin system, so check measures on a π-system.

*Makes the theorem clean to state or prove.* MSC 28A05 (Classes of sets (Borel fields, σ-rings, etc.), measurable sets, Suslin sets, analytic sets), 28A12. arXiv math.CA, math.PR. Level 2: later undergraduate.

**Definition.** A Dynkin system on Ω contains Ω and is closed under differences of nested members and under increasing countable unions, exactly the rules that agreement of two probability measures obeys. A π-system is a nonempty family closed under pairwise intersections.

**Problem.** Two probability measures that agree on a π-system agree on the σ-algebra it generates. Hence a law on the real line is fixed by its distribution function, and Lebesgue measure is the only Borel measure giving each interval its length.

**How the definition helps.** The sets where two probability measures agree form a Dynkin system, so the π-λ theorem extends agreement from a π-system to its σ-algebra (our own). The half-lines ending at real points form a π-system that generates the Borel sets (our own). For interval length, restrict both measures to bounded intervals, normalize there, and exhaust the line (our own).

**Problem.** Let the σ-algebra on Y be generated by a family 𝒢. A map from a space with a σ-algebra to Y is measurable exactly when each set in 𝒢 has a measurable preimage.

**Problem.** Finite signed measures on a fixed measurable space form a real Banach space under the total variation norm. On a locally compact Hausdorff space, the real dual of C₀ identifies with the finite regular signed Borel measures.

**Remember:** Separate the signs so neither measure can hide mass behind the other.

*Rules out counterexamples.* MSC 28A12 (Contents, measures, outer measures, capacities). arXiv math.CA. Level 2: later undergraduate.

### Carathéodory's measurability condition builds additivity into the definition

**Remember:** A measurable set cuts every set into pieces whose sizes add.

*True by definition.* MSC 28A12 (Contents, measures, outer measures, capacities), 28A05. arXiv math.CA. Level 2: later undergraduate.

**Definition.** Let μ\* be an outer measure on X, a monotone and countably subadditive size function on all subsets with μ\*(∅) = 0. A set E is Carathéodory measurable when μ\*(A) = μ\*(A ∩ E) + μ\*(A ∖ E) for every subset A of X.

**Problem.** The measurable sets form a σ-algebra (a family of sets closed under complements and countable unions). On these sets μ\* is countably additive, so it is a measure.

**How the definition helps.** The splitting identity is additivity tested on every set A, and it is symmetric in E and its complement (our own). For disjoint measurable Eₙ, repeated splitting gives μ\*(A) ≥ ∑ₙ μ\*(A ∩ Eₙ) + μ\*(A ∖ ⋃ₙEₙ), and subadditivity makes the union measurable (our own). Splitting twice gives finite unions, disjointifying then gives all countable unions, and A = ⋃ₙEₙ gives countable additivity (our own).

**Wikipedia:** [Outer measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outer_measure), [Carathéodory's extension theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carath%C3%A9odory%27s_extension_theorem). It says: “The purpose of constructing an outer measure on all subsets of X is to pick out a class of subsets (to be called measurable) in such a way as to satisfy the countable additivity property.”

**Problem.** Two-dimensional Lebesgue measure is the completion of the product of one-dimensional Lebesgue measure with itself. In a complete space, any function equal almost everywhere to a measurable function is itself measurable.

*True by definition.* MSC 28A12 (Contents, measures, outer measures, capacities). arXiv math.CA. Level 2: later undergraduate.

**Definition.** Lebesgue outer measure on ℝ is the infimum of total lengths of countable interval covers, while Jordan outer content allows only finite covers. **Problem.** Every countable set of reals has Lebesgue measure zero. The rationals in \[0, 1\] are such a set, but they are not Jordan measurable.

**Definition.** A set E is countably n-rectifiable if countably many Lipschitz images of subsets of ℝⁿ cover E outside an n-dimensional Hausdorff null set. At x, positive lower n-density means ℋⁿ(E ∩ B(x,r)) ≥ crⁿ for some c &gt; 0 and all sufficiently small r &gt; 0 (our own).

**Problem.** For a measurable set E of finite n-dimensional Hausdorff measure in a complete metric space, rectifiability is equivalent to positive lower density and a unique measured tangent at almost every point. That tangent is a normed ℝⁿ with normalized Hausdorff measure.

**How the definition helps.** Rectifiability uses distances and Hausdorff measure, so both charts and measured tangents make sense without an ambient Euclidean space (our own). The paper’s Theorem 1.1 proves the equivalence by relating the tangent condition to small-scale bi-Lipschitz approximations.

**Paper or page:** [Characterising rectifiable metric spaces using tangent spaces](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.12371v3). It says: “We also introduce a generalisation of Preiss's tangent measures that is suitable for the setting of arbitrary metric spaces and formulate our characterisation in terms of tangent measures.”

### Countable tests fix weak density versions

**Remember:** Each vector tests separately; countably many tests share one null set.

*Rules out counterexamples.* MSC 28B05 (Vector-valued set functions, measures and integrals). arXiv math.FA. Level 4: research.

**Definition.** A weak-star density of a measure m with values in the dual X\* is a function ζ to X\* that is tested one vector at a time. For each v in X, t ↦ ζ(t)(v) is integrable and ∫<sub>A</sub> ζ(t)(v) dμ(t) = m(A)(v) for every measurable A (our own).

**Problem.** On a finite Lebesgue interval, two weak-star densities of the same vector measure agree almost everywhere if the original Banach space is separable.

**How the definition helps.** For each v, two densities give scalar functions that agree outside a null set that depends on v (our own). For separable X, the union of these null sets over a countable dense set of v is null, and continuity gives ζ₁ = ζ₂ off it (our own). Without separability, on ℓ¹(\[0,1\]) the density ζ(t) = evaluation at coordinate t represents zero yet has norm one everywhere (our own).

**Paper or page:** [Global entropy solutions to a degenerate parabolic-parabolic chemotaxis system for flux-limited dispersal](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.03887v3). It says: “If X is separable, then any ζ₁ and ζ₂ that satisfy the conditions on ζ coincide a.e. in {g &gt; 0}.”

### Prevalence gives infinite-dimensional spaces a measure meaning of almost every

**Remember:** A shy set is invisible to one probe, from every position.

*Makes the theorem clean to state or prove.* MSC 28C20 (Set functions and measures and integrals in infinite-dimensional spaces (Wiener measure, Gaussian measure, etc.)), 37C20. arXiv math.CA, math.DS. Level 4: research.

**Definition.** In a complete metric vector space, a Borel set B is shy if one compactly supported Borel probability μ gives zero mass to every translate v + B. Subsets of such sets are also shy, and the complement of a shy set is prevalent (our own).

**Problem.** Subsets, translates and countable unions of shy sets are shy, and prevalent sets are dense. In this sense, almost every Cᵏ map of ℝⁿ into itself has only hyperbolic periodic points, for integers k ≥ 1.

**How the definition helps.** One probe measure works for every translate at once, so shyness plays the role of Lebesgue measure zero without a Lebesgue measure on the space (our own). The prevalence theorem of Hunt, Sauer and Yorke gives the closure and density properties (our own). Their hyperbolicity theorem uses a probe supported on a finite-dimensional family of perturbations (our own).

**Wikipedia:** [Prevalent and shy sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prevalent_and_shy_sets), [Generic property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generic_property). It says: “the notions of prevalence and shyness are notions of almost everywhere and measure zero that are well-suited to the study of infinite-dimensional spaces”

**Sources:**

- [B. R. Hunt, T. Sauer, J. A. Yorke, Prevalence: a translation-invariant almost every on infinite-dimensional spaces, Bulletin of the American Mathematical Society 27 (1992) 217–238](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0273-0979-1992-00328-2)
