Joe Hudson is an executive coach and the founder of Art of Accomplishment. He has coached leaders at OpenAI and other large tech firms. His podcast with Brett Kistler holds most of his method, and it is free.

This post puts the most useful parts in one place and sorts them by what you are trying not to feel. Topics link to a matching short video or full interview, and [every video found](#every-video) is listed at the end. Steps are paraphrased from his episodes, essays and the one-page guides he posts on X. Other teachers are named where a practice is theirs.

## The short version

1. **Find the loop.** Name the feeling you avoid. Then see how your way of avoiding it brings it back.
2. **Feel it on purpose.** Do Emotional Inquiry once or twice a day. Meet the feeling in your body, with wonder, and do not try to change it.
3. **If you cannot love the feeling, love your resistance to it.** Resistance is the place to start, not a mistake.
4. **Run small experiments.** Do not take his word for it. Try one thing for a week and see.
5. **Stay safe.** Never aim anger at a person without their consent. If you feel flooded, stop and rest.

## Read this first

This is coaching, not treatment. Hudson says that if you get overwhelmed, care for your nervous system comes first: rest, ground yourself and slow down. He advises one feeling at a time, perhaps twice a day. He also says hard early work, such as childhood chaos, should be done with skilled people, not alone.

If you have trauma, or you get lost in these feelings or feel outside your body, work with a licensed therapist. If you think about harming yourself or someone else, contact a crisis line or emergency service now.

## The core method

**The Golden Algorithm.** The emotion you avoid comes back through the way you avoid it. Map it for one problem that repeats:

1. Name the problem and what happens in it.
2. Name one emotion you may be avoiding.
3. Name what you do to avoid it.
4. Trace how that brings the same emotion back. You skip the gym to dodge guilt, and skipping makes more guilt.
5. Look for more emotions. There is often more than one.
6. Welcome the emotion. If you welcome it only to make it leave, you are still resisting.

Other loops he names: you dodge conflict by pleasing people, and end up with conflict inside. You dodge feeling weak by dominating people, and they leave.

- **Find it in ten decisions.** List ten decisions from today. Beside each, write the feeling it was meant to bring or avoid. See which you avoid most and which you chase.
- **Follow every "I can't".** Each "I can't" or "that's too hard" marks a feeling. "I can't ask for a raise" may be a way to avoid rejection.

**Emotional Inquiry.** This is his main tool. Call up the feeling, from a memory if you need to. Find it in your body. Ask where its centre is, what colour and how dense it is, and whether it moves. Keep coming back to it, as a small child looks at a frog. Do not analyse it or try to shift it. The guided version is about 8 minutes. He suggests one to three times a day.

**Serious, not literal.** Your body reacts as if the story in a feeling were true. Respect the reaction and do not believe the story. He compares this to an actor who plays rage in full and still knows it is a part.

**If you cannot welcome it, welcome the resistance.** Fighting your resistance is more resistance. Put your attention on the part that resists. Be curious. Let it say what it wants. Stay as you would with a scared child, with no hurry for it to change.

**When you are triggered.** Pause. Ask when you first felt this way. Then do Emotional Inquiry on what comes.

**Audit your judgments.** List everywhere you judged someone in the last 24 hours. For each, ask what you would have to feel if you could not judge. Then write what is good in that person that the judgment hid.

**VIEW.** This is the state you bring to any feeling or talk.

- **Vulnerability.** Let it touch you.
- **Impartiality.** Do not try to change it.
- **Empathy.** Keep yourself company, and stay out of the story.
- **Wonder.** Stay in the question with no need for an answer.

**Four quick tests.**

- **Resist, then release.** Take your strongest feeling. Resist it fully for a moment. Then drop the resistance and welcome it. Compare the two.
- **Bracing or embracing.** Close your eyes, breathe and ask which one you are doing. He once did this about 10 times a day.
- **Go and feel.** Sit 10 to 15 minutes with something you avoid. Then ask what was so bad, and what kept you from being destroyed.
- **Feel and not-feel.** Clamp down on every emotion. Then release and allow all of it. Repeat at least once a day for a week, and make the feeling phases longer.

**Signs of a feeling you have not felt.** Your thoughts loop. A decision stays unclear. You think in either/or terms. You judge someone. You get defensive. You rush. You try to control everything.

**Signs you learned to hide feelings as a child.** You overthink when a hard feeling comes. You get distracted or confused when you try to feel. You judge other people's big feelings, even their joy.

**How to run an experiment.**

- Make it short, often a week. Stop early if the answer is clear.
- Start where you feel curious or in pain.
- Test something that goes against what you believe.
- Ask what will happen. Do not set a goal.
- Run one at a time. If it is too big, shrink it. He cut a 30-minute daily breathwork practice to one 3-minute session.
- If it turns into a "should", change it. If you use it to control yourself, it stops working.

## His principles

**Five for running a company.** He says these are how he runs his own firm, and that no company needs more than five.

- **Open the drawer.** Raise the frightening issue early and with care. This is no licence for cruelty or for drama.
- **Everything is an iteration.** Treat work as tests, and ask for advice instead of permission. Sloppy work is not a test, and every test needs a clear aim.
- **Enjoyment is efficiency.** Work slowly enough to work well, and learn to like the dull parts. This is no excuse to dodge stress or hard jobs.
- **Empower everyone.** Whoever owns a decision owns its result. Do not take people's problems from them, or hand them yours.
- **Act from connection.** Work from care for yourself and others. Niceness that avoids conflict does not count, and nor does giving until you resent it.

**Head, heart and gut.** He teaches change on three levels.

- **Head.** Your sense of time and of self grow wider. It gets harder to take things personally.
- **Heart.** You can feel everything without shutting down or collapsing. He uses joy as the measure.
- **Gut.** Pleasure teaches your nervous system that you are safe, so you stop bracing. The measure is how much you can enjoy simply being alive.

He pictures emotional growth as a horseshoe. Infants feel freely. Then we repress. Then we manage feelings. Last, we return to feeling freely, without taking it all personally.

**Beliefs he returns to.**

- Self-improvement assumes you are broken. Self-discovery assumes something is hidden. He prefers the second.
- Feelings are not problems to solve. They ask to be heard.
- Thinking, talking and writing about a feeling are not the same as feeling it.
- What you shame, you repeat.
- The feeling is rarely what hurts. Resisting it is.
- To change a habit, change what holds it. He compares this to water, which you cannot grip but can pour into a new container.
- Letting go comes when it is ready. Telling yourself to do it does not work.

## What each feeling is telling you

This table condenses his three-part guide. Read it as his view of each signal, and do the feeling first.

| Feeling | What he says it signals | Ask or do |
|---|---|---|
| Sadness | Something is ending or no longer works | Stay with the ache. Say goodbye to what is over |
| Anger | What you care about, and where a line is | What boundary do I want? What is not working? |
| Fear | Excitement without breath, or something that needs care | Breathe. How do I look after this? |
| Disgust | You were made to take in something you did not want | What was forced on me? How do I get clear of it? |
| Anxiety | Something you know needs doing and have not done. The task may be to feel something | What one step would settle this? |
| Guilt | You have stopped listening to what you most want | How do I want to do this next time? |
| Jealousy | You want something and push the want away | Let yourself want it. What do I want here? |
| Shame | You feel cut off and have left part of yourself behind | What can I connect to right now? |
| Contempt | A trait you have not seen in yourself | Where do I have this too? |
| Love | You feel safe enough for old patterns to surface | Stay when old wounds flare. Do not read them as proof it is wrong |
| Joy | Arrives once the other feelings are let in | Welcome them all. Do not chase it |

- **The tiger.** Close your eyes. Imagine a tiger closing in on you, with no way out. Hear its feet and its breath. Now ask how much it weighs. Wonder and fear are hard to hold together. Use it for lesser fears, such as being fired, and not for real danger.
- **Walk through the outcome.** Pick the fear. Picture the steps before, during and after it, again and again. Feel what it would bring.
- **Say the scary truth.** Ask what scary thing is true for you right now. Then say it out loud.
- **Shake and make sound.** Let the body shake and let sounds out. He recommends doing this with others.
- **Check the story.** If fear is moving, the story changes and relief follows. If the story stays the same, you may be feeling it only to get rid of it.
- **Name your role.** He calls this the Fear Triangle. Feeling stuck means you are the victim. Feeling angry and alone means you are the bully. Feeling obligated means you are the savior.
- **Ask what you defend.** What are you defending? What is the you that you are defending? What makes you think it can be destroyed?

- **Stop, invite, love.** Each time shame comes, stop. Invite it. Love it. Tell it that it can come back at any time. Move when you are ready. He calls this the fastest way through.
- **Look under it.** Ask what you would have to feel if you could not feel ashamed right now. Then feel that. This is also the tool for regret that replays.
- **Look for it in the habit.** If you keep doing something you hate, look for the shame around it.
- **Find the want.** Describe what you truly want and feel the desire inside the shame.
- **Find the double bind.** Name a want. Ask if you also want its opposite, and if neither feels okay.
- **Speak it among warm people.** Say the shameful thing to people who care for you. He calls this the best route.
- **Drop the hot potato.** In a fight, only one person has to stop. Face each other. Say that nothing in you wants the other to feel ashamed or bad right now. Notice any wish to shame them.

## Guilt

**Short videos (4):** [If Others' Anger Makes You Feel You Did Something Wrong ↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=djOMmAkd6k0) (10 min) · [How to Apologize in A Way That Transforms You ↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ez13eCKqlMw) (5 min) · ["I'm Not Responsible For How You Feel" (Coaching with Joe) ↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xVXOesjPE3k) (12 min) · [I'm Tired Of Taking Responsibility ↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Si3pYvzF06Q) (12 min)

**Full interviews and episodes (1):** [The Upright Apology ↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fdsUsWBXHDQ) (34 min)

- **The upright apology.** Sit or stand with your back straight. Say what happened and which part you are responsible for. Say that it is not how you want to be, and that you apologise. Avoid the word "you". Do not try to change how they feel.
- **Twenty apologies.** List the times you were passive-aggressive and make about 20 upright apologies. His guess, which he did not measure, is that this cuts the habit by a third or so.
- **Apologise to yourself.** For a bad habit, apologise to yourself each day for the harm it does.
- **Ask what it covers.** If you could not feel guilty, what else would you notice or want? He treats guilt as a door back to your wants.
- **Use guilt as a check.** If you feel guilty or "dirty" after a talk, you were probably trying to control it.
- **Refuse the guilt trip.** Say you are not interested in being guilt-tripped. Or name it and ask what makes them do it right now.

This covers frustration and rage too. Read the [evidence](#what-the-evidence-says) before you start. It is the part of his method that research disputes most.

**Four signs it is held in.** You attack yourself. It leaks sideways as sarcasm, lateness or guilt trips. It builds and then blows up. Or it collapses into feeling stuck and low.

**His rules.** Do not aim it at anyone without their permission. Do not hurt yourself or others, and do not break things. If you try to scare or control someone with it, that is manipulation.

1. **Find a safe place.** A pillow, a car, a beach, or a room with loud music.
2. **Set a time.** Tell yourself you will listen to the anger for 15 to 20 minutes. He suggests 40 for people who are low.
3. **Keep part of you outside it.** Treat it as a role you perform well. If you tend to shut down, lean into the story a little first.
4. **Use the body and voice.** Yell, open the chest and make sounds. Heavy music can help.
5. **Tell your body it is safe.** No one is attacking you and you are not hurting anyone.
6. **Check that it is done.** Real completion brings relief and clarity about you, not a verdict on them.

**Tests for clean anger.** You are not trying to control anyone. You are not targeting or blaming anyone. It leaves you clearer and more open. Expect more anger for a while when you stop holding it in, as when a kink comes out of a hose.

**If it goes wrong.** If you collapse, use your will and repeat a short line such as "No. I won't collapse." If you leave your body or lose track of time, return to the role or to the timed listening. If it keeps coming back fast, look for fear, hurt or grief under it.

**Also useful.**

- **Commute anger.** Get angry alone in the car each night, with no one there. He reports change in two or three weeks for one client.
- **Announce and leave.** Tell your family you are angry, that you do not want to take it out on them, and that you will drive for 20 minutes.
- **Release, then talk.** Move the anger first. Have the talk after.
- **Ask what it cares about.** What do I care about here? What line was crossed? What clarity is it looking for?

Depression is a medical condition. The hosts say treatment can help and do not argue against it. Treat these as additions to care, not a swap for it.

Hudson maps low mood on four levels: harsh self-talk, held-in anger or sadness, a body always on watch, and being cut off from people.

- **Attention in the body.** Keep 10% of your attention in your body as much as you can for two to three weeks. He warns it can be intense.
- **Feel the numbness itself.** Brett's sequence: treat distraction as a fear signal, feel the numbness, wait for anxiety, then name the fears.
- **Debate the top 20.** Write your 20 most persistent harsh thoughts and argue with them.
- **Change "should" to "want".** Note each "should" and try "want".

## Problems these feelings cause

**The voice in your head.**

- **The 5x5x5.** For five days, write out the voice for 5 minutes a day. For the next five days, try 5 different replies each day. Examples: ask it to treat you with more respect, or tell it you see it is scared and you are here. Then look in your journal for where it was false or contradicted itself.
- **Play with it.** Sing its lines. Give it a silly name. Hold it like a child in a tantrum.
- **The boss test.** Say each harsh line out loud. Then picture a boss talking to you that way.
- **Look at the other 97.** He says you do 100 things a day and pick three that make you bad. Notice the good in the rest.
- **Say "ouch".** Listen to the voice for 20 minutes a day for a week. Each time it is mean, say "ouch" out loud.

Watch, short: [1 Word That Changes Your Inner Critic (Full Guide) ↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZeIShRO_8NE) (12 min); full: [Who Is The Voice In Your Head ↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IS_UwKfAi9c) (41 min).

**Procrastination.**

- **The Double Blind.** For 7 days, each time you put off a task, name what you feel now and what you fear you would feel if you did it. Do Emotional Inquiry on both. The published steps jump from 2 to 4, so one may be missing.
- **Enjoy first.** For a week, sit down with two aims: enjoy it first, finish it second. If you are not enjoying it, stop and find a way that you do.
- **Ask one question.** How much would you procrastinate if you could not shame yourself?
- **Double the enjoyment.** Ask how you could enjoy the task twice as much. List 10 experiments and run them.
- **Three things.** Keep three items that matter. Ask what would make the list easier or not needed.

Watch, short: [Watch Him Dissolve Procrastination In 11 Minutes (Coaching With Joe Hudson) ↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N65NPYRN8z8) (11 min); full: [I Don't Procrastinate Anymore - Here's What Changed (Joe Hudson) ↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Qrw4jBtKJqg) (32 min).

**Forcing yourself.** The signs are constant shoulds, leaning on willpower, a life that needs managing, and resisting goals you chose.

- **Two columns.** On the left, list all you forced yourself to do today. Put a hand on your heart. For each item, ask what your heart wants. Wait for the answer and write it on the right.
- **Catch it.** Notice the word "should", and any tightness in your body.
- **Come back.** What do I want here? What do my head, heart and gut each want? What would I do with no shoulds?
- **Slow the reach.** Before a habit such as coffee, find the feeling just before you reach for it and the one you get after. See what you are avoiding or chasing.

**Doing everything yourself.** He traces this to a child who had to look after the adults. It can pass for high agency, and he likes agency. Taken too far, it brings burnout and broken relationships.

- **The signs.** You carry other people's feelings. You rarely say what you want. You ask for help in ways that put people off. You distrust authority and are uneasy holding it.

**Money.**

- **Ask what you are chasing.** He says it is usually safety, approval or freedom, which money cannot fully give.
- **Check if it runs the show.** You feel shame at having too little. You think more will make you better or safe. You judge people with more or less.
- **Hating it counts too.** Rejecting money still gives it power over you. In his twenties he lived on under $12,000 a year to prove he could. He calls that the same obsession. This is a story.
- **Trace the charge.** When money stirs you up, close your eyes and find the feeling in your body. Ask when you first felt it, and what you called it before you knew about money.
- **Name what you have.** Take 10 minutes a day for two weeks to name gratitude for what you already have, best with a partner. He and his wife did this while in debt, and he says they were out of debt within a year. This is a story too.

**Purpose.** He says asking "what is my purpose?" is mostly self-abuse, and that purpose is not found in the future.

- **Try it before you quit.** For 30 days, act fully as yourself at work. In each meeting, ask how you would listen and speak if you were on purpose.
- **Set a chime.** Have a timer ring seven to nine times a day. At each ring, take 20 seconds to live that way now.

**Decisions.**

- **See past the false end.** He says fear shows up as either/or thinking and as treating this choice as your last. Keep imagining past the moment you dread: and then, and then.
- **Think in portfolios.** Judge a year of decisions the way a fund judges its holdings. No single one has to be right.
- **Audit self-abandonment.** Look back over the last week or two months. Where did you sense this was wrong and do it anyway? What is creating resentment? He reads resentment as a sign you abandoned yourself.
- **Notice when you need reassurance.** He treats each need for it as a small self-abandonment.

- **Head, heart, gut.** Say what each one says about the choice.
- **Fortune or peace.** Imagine having the outcome and being miserable. Then imagine losing all of it and being joyful. See which you would choose.
- **Know what you know.** What do you know? What do you not know? What do you need to know next?
- **Write your own principles.** Keep to about five. Test each for five days, and define it by what it is not as well as what it is. A good set gets you where you want to go about 80% of the time and solves about 80% of your problems. Run hard decisions through each one.

**Hard talks and boundaries.**

- **Ask how and what, not why.** "Why" sounds like a charge.
- **Say what you held back.** Maybe one thing a day.
- **Book the time.** Keep a monthly block, about three hours. Ask which talks you are not having. Then call those people.
- **State what you will do.** A boundary is not a demand. Say that if the yelling goes on you will leave, and say when you will check back.
- **Test it first.** Picture saying it. A good boundary keeps your heart open whatever they answer.
- **Name your agenda.** Say "I notice I am trying to fix you", and ask how that is for them.
- **Know what pleasing is.** He defines it as the fear of someone's reaction, more than a wish to be liked. Care ends where their mood starts to decide your happiness.
- **Start absurdly small.** When asked where to eat, say "pizza" and not "anything". List where you did not own your truth today, and practise on the smallest ones.
- **Tell the whole story.** Replace a hedged request with all that is true: what you want, what it costs, and that you are scared to ask.
- **Admit it to your partner.** Say you notice you were scared of them and were not really loving them, and that you are sorry.
- **Go a little further in.** When you cringe or freeze, go into it a little more on purpose. He and his co-host say the resistance loosens.
- **Say "I disagree".** For a week, say it each time you do not agree. It may come out defensive or superior at first. Keep going until it comes out clean. He gives this for people-pleasing.
- **Give no advice for a day.** Fix no one. Listen, and ask only questions that start with how or what. When it is uncomfortable, note the feeling you are avoiding.
- **Before you leave a relationship.** For a few weeks, be fully yourself and speak your truth without defending. He says this often turns things around, and that if it does not, your next relationship is better for it.

**Choosing a partner.** You will both change. So he asks whether someone will grow alongside you, more than whether they suit you today. Look for a person who can have hard talks, is curious about their own growth, and solves problems with you without keeping score.

Watch, short: [Before You End a Relationship, Ask These 14 Questions ↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7i1SvYJj0TE) (10 min); full: [Finding a Romantic Partner ↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zvjTWEqUMnU) (35 min).

**Raising children.** This is his account of his own family.

- **No punishment.** He says it teaches children to punish themselves and to doubt their own goodness. He and his wife did not use timeouts or take things away.
- **Own your anger.** Anger aimed at a small child is punishment. Anger you own, with a child who knows they are safe, is different.
- **Consequences come from life and from limits.** Do not shield children from what follows their actions. Set limits early, and state what you will do, not what you will do to them.
- **Repair.** He apologised to a teenage daughter for a pattern she had learned from him.

**Teams.**

- **Five-star meetings.** Rate each meeting from 1 to 5 by the energy it gives. Aim for a week of only five-star meetings within a month or two.
- **Ask the room.** What are we not talking about? What are you scared to say?
- **Ask what doubles it.** What would double our results? What would double our enjoyment?
- **Open with thanks.** Start meetings with a round of gratitude.
- **Set conditions, not orders.** He says control kills creativity. Describe what success needs and leave the how to them.
- **Watch for silence.** He says the most dangerous person in a company is the one who never points out problems.
- **Let the team hold itself to account.** If people answer only to the boss, old family dynamics land on the boss.
- **Look at pace.** He says CEOs overrate the quality of each decision and underrate how fast they make and delegate them.
- **Use the no doctrine.** Do not sell your idea. Ask people to tell you everything broken about it, and mine each objection for a better answer.
- **Own the projection.** When you blame someone, find how you do the same thing. He says you almost always do.
- **Practise being triggered.** He has executive teams set each other off on purpose and work through it, so hard talks get easier.
- **Require experiments.** Each person at his company runs three to five experiments a quarter on how they do their job.
- **Tell a hard truth early.** He says avoiding conflict with staff ends in revolt or in people leaving.
- **Blame the cause, not the person.** Ask what made it not get done, what changes, and how the team can help.

## Where his advice differs

He teaches by experiment, so his numbers shift. Treat a dose as a place to start.

- **Moving big feelings alone.** He says both to practise anger alone first and not to do this alone. A careful reading is to start small alone, and to do deep release with a grounded person.
- **Shame.** One episode says to love the shame itself. Others say to feel what is under it. Try each.
- **Naming feelings.** He suggests naming your emotion 5 to 10 times a day. He also warns that a label can become a way not to feel. Name it, then drop into the body.
- **Writing.** He says writing about a feeling is not feeling it, and he also assigns journals. Use writing to find a feeling, then go to the body.
- **Why questions.** He says to avoid "why", and sometimes asks it himself.
- **How much.** Emotional Inquiry runs from daily to three times a day. Gratitude is 7 or 10 minutes. Replies to the inner voice change daily, every two days, weekly or five times a day. He has said both five principles and "five, seven".

## What the evidence says

Most of this rests on Hudson's coaching and on reports from students. A web search found no trial or peer-reviewed test of his method. One student of his Master Class, [Andrew Conner ↗](https://andrewconner.com/the-art-of-accomplishment-master-class-review/), later added that a sincere testimonial is not evidence, and that the gains had mellowed.

**Venting is where the research pushes back.** A [2024 review of 154 studies ↗](https://news.osu.edu/breathe-dont-vent-turning-down-the-heat-is-key-to-managing-anger/) with about 10,000 people found that activities which lower arousal reduce anger, such as slow breathing and meditation. Activities that raise arousal did not help overall. An [earlier experiment ↗](https://public.websites.umich.edu/~bbushman/PSPB02.pdf) found that venting while thinking about the offender raised anger and aggression.

Those studies test anger reduction, and not Hudson's protocol with its stated rules. Still, if your anger grows after you move it, or you act on it more, stop and try the calming route. His own test is the right one: you should end with relief and clarity.

**Check the science he cites.** His X posts lean on research, and those claims were not checked for this post. They include his line that people who go into their depression rarely relapse. One [post on the heart ↗](https://x.com/FU_joehudson/status/1946221733037682822) shows a paper on transplant patients taking on donors' traits. That paper is a set of interviews with 10 recipients, and it cannot show cause. He also says CBT works mainly on the head. CBT has been tested in many trials, and his method has not.

**Writing has modest support.** A [meta-analysis of 146 randomised studies ↗](https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/2006-frattaroli.pdf) found a small positive effect from writing about hard experiences.
