Some stories get told so often that nobody checks them. This page checks them. For each story it finds the earliest telling it can, and the event, study or record the story claims to report. Both are quoted, and each has an archived copy.

A story counts as false if the event did not happen as told: it was invented, it was changed in a way that carries its lesson, or it is told about the wrong people, place or field. Whether the lesson still holds is a separate question, and each entry answers it separately.

The rules were fixed and committed before any research began. The [method, the data and every change to the plan ↗](https://github.com/yujieteo/site/tree/main/audits/anecdotes) are public. Retellings and debunking articles were used only to find sources, never as evidence.

**This is a draft.** 11 of the 53 candidate stories are checked. Nothing is ranked or scored yet: the counts of how often each story is told, and the two raters' scores, come after every story is checked, so that the scores cannot be shaped by the counts.

## False or misleading

### Five caged monkeys learn to beat any monkey that climbs a ladder for a banana, after the hosed originals are gone

**As usually told:** Scientists put five monkeys in a cage with a banana at the top of a ladder. Whenever a monkey climbed toward it, all the monkeys were sprayed with cold water, so they learned to beat any monkey that tried. The monkeys were then replaced one at a time, until none had ever been sprayed, yet they still beat any monkey that climbed the ladder. Nobody knew why; that was just how things were done.

**The usual lesson:** Groups keep enforcing rules long after the reason for them is gone, and newcomers learn the rule without the reason.

**What happened:** No experiment matching the story was found. The earliest telling found, Hamel and Prahalad, Competing for the Future (1994), credits 'a friend of ours' and gives no source; it has four monkeys and a pole. Later retellings name a 1967 study by Gordon R. Stephenson. Stephenson's own description (1968 congress paper, published 1969) shows a different study: monkeys in pairs, one monkey punished with an air blast when it touched a plastic kitchen object. Sometimes the naive partner then avoided the object, and once the trained monkey pulled the naive one away by the hind legs. In other pairs the trained monkey lost its fear after watching the naive one handle the object. There were no five monkeys, no ladder, no banana, no water spray and no chain of replacements.

**Verdict:** Changed: it happened, but a detail the lesson needs is false. The study that retellers name as the experiment (Stephenson 1967) did happen and did show that monkeys can pass on the avoidance of an object to a naive partner. But the details that the lesson needs are false: there was no group of five, no replacement of the monkeys one by one, and no finding that the rule outlived every trained monkey. In some pairs the fear passed the other way, from the naive monkey to the trained one, and it faded. The ladder, banana and cold-water spray are also not in the study.

**Does the lesson hold?** Not established. Laboratory studies of arbitrary traditions, where members are replaced one at a time, find that a tradition persists for some replacements and then fades toward what is useful. That supports a partial and temporary version of the lesson, not the strong claim that a rule lasts unchanged after everyone who knew its reason is gone.

**Earliest telling found:** Competing for the Future (Boston: Harvard Business School Press, 1994); scan is the 1996 paperback, item competingforfut000hame, Gary Hamel and C. K. Prahalad, 1994. [Source ↗](https://openlibrary.org/search/inside.json?q=%22once+described+an+experiment+with+monkeys%22&limit=100) · [Archived copy ↗](https://archive.org/details/competingforfut000hame)

> A friend of ours ... Four monkeys were put

**Original record:** Stephenson's own summary of the 1967 study that later retellings cite., Gordon R. Stephenson, 1968 (paper given); 1969 (published). [Source ↗](https://openlibrary.org/search/inside.json?q=%22control+studies+were+done%22+%22whenever+it+attempted%22+%22in+the+study+were%22+%22the+naive%2C+grabbed%22+%22until+after+it+observed%22&limit=100) · [Archived copy ↗](https://archive.org/details/proceedings0003inte)

**Original record:** Stephenson's description of the conditioning: an air blast, not water, and one monkey of a pair., Gordon R. Stephenson, 1968 (paper given); 1969 (published). [Source ↗](https://openlibrary.org/search/inside.json?q=%22air+blast+whenever%22&limit=100) · [Archived copy ↗](https://archive.org/details/proceedings0003inte)

> specific object by punishing it with an ... it attempted to manipulate the object

**Evidence on the lesson:** Baum, Richerson, Efferson and Paciotti, 'Cultural evolution in laboratory microsocieties including traditions of rule giving and rule following', Evolution and Human Behavior 25 (2004), describing Jacobs and Campbell (1961) and their own results. The class is changed and not invented because the record that retellers point to (Stephenson) exists and shows a related but different event; the earliest telling found (1994) does not itself cite Stephenson, so if that link is rejected, the class would be unsupported.

### NASA spent millions on a pen that writes in space; the Russians used a pencil

**As usually told:** When NASA found that ballpoint pens would not work in zero gravity, it spent years and millions (in some versions $12 billion) of taxpayers' money developing a pen that writes in space. The Soviet cosmonauts faced the same problem and simply used a pencil.

**The usual lesson:** Expensive high-technology solutions are often waste when a cheap, simple tool would do the job.

**What happened:** Early NASA astronauts did use pencils. In 1965 NASA bought 34 mechanical pencils at $128.89 each, which caused public criticism. Paul C. Fisher of the Fisher Pen Company developed a pressurized ballpoint pen with no NASA funding, reportedly investing about $1 million of the company's own money, and filed a patent on it in his own name on 19 May 1965. NASA tested the pen and in 1967 chose it for Apollo, buying about 400 pens at about $6 each. The Soviet Union, whose cosmonauts had used grease pencils, bought 100 Fisher pens and 1,000 cartridges in February 1969.

**Verdict:** Invented: it did not happen. NASA did not spend millions developing a space pen; a private company developed it with its own money and sold it to NASA for a few dollars a pen, and the Soviets bought the same pen.

**Does the lesson hold?** Not established. No study was found that tests how often costly high-technology solutions are waste compared with simple ones. In this case the simple tool was not clearly better: NASA's own pencils had cost $128.89 each and drew criticism, and the Soviets later bought the pen too.

**Earliest telling found:** LaughLab: The Scientific Quest for the World's Funniest Joke (London: Arrow Books, 2002), page 198 of the scan by the search index, told as a joke, LaughLab project (book; the archive.org record gives no author), 2002. ### A cinema flashed "Eat popcorn" and "Drink Coca-Cola" on screen and sales rose

**As usually told:** In 1957 a cinema in New Jersey flashed the words "Eat popcorn" and "Drink Coca-Cola" on the screen during films, too fast for the audience to see. Over six weeks, popcorn sales rose by 57.5% and Coca-Cola sales by 18.1%. The test, by the market researcher James Vicary, is told as proof that hidden messages make people buy.

**The usual lesson:** Hidden messages that people cannot consciously see can make large numbers of them buy a product.

**What happened:** On 12 September 1957 James Vicary showed reporters in New York a film with hidden "Drink Coca-Cola" flashes and said that a six-week test in a New Jersey cinema had raised popcorn sales by 57.5% and Coca-Cola sales by 18.1%. He did not name the cinema and gave only attendance and percentage figures, no sales records. In December 1957 Motion Picture Daily found that the cinema was the Fort Lee Theatre, Fort Lee, New Jersey, and that Vicary's company had put its equipment there for six weeks in the summer of 1957. The executives in charge of the theatre told the paper that the device had no effect on refreshment stand sales and that sales stayed at the theatre's usual average. The next day the circuit's president, B. S. Moss, said he was satisfied that the method could help sales, but only on figures that Vicary's own company had given him, and he said more tests were needed.

**Verdict:** Changed: it happened, but a detail the lesson needs is false. The sales rise: the theatre's own executives reported at the time that sales did not change, and the only figures for a rise came from Vicary's company; no sales records from the test were found published.

**Does the lesson hold?** Not established. Laboratory studies find that a hidden brand cue can shift choice a little, but only in people who already need that kind of product (thirsty or tired). No study shows a sales rise of the size Vicary claimed among ordinary audiences.

**Earliest telling found:** Advertising Age, vol. 28, no. 37, 16 September 1957, "'Persuaders' Get Deeply 'Hidden' Tool: Subliminal Projection", Advertising Age (unsigned report, dateline New York, 12 September 1957), 1957-09-16. [Source ↗](https://archive.org/details/sim_advertising-age_1957-09-16_28_37)

> Mr. Vicary reported that he recently tested the "invisible commercial" in a New Jersey movie theater. The tests ran for six weeks, during which time some 45,000 persons attended the theater. Two advertising messages were projected—one urging the audience to eat popcorn, the other suggesting, "Drink Coca-Cola." According to Mr. Vicary, the "invisible commercial" increased popcorn sales by 57.5% and ... sales by 18.1%.

**Original record:** Motion Picture Daily report naming the cinema, quoting Vicary's press release, and giving the theatre executives' account of sales, Motion Picture Daily (unsigned), 1957-12-16. [Source ↗](https://archive.org/details/motionpicturedai82unse_0)

> "The invisible advertising increased popcorn sales on the average of 57.5%, and increased sales of Coca-Cola on the average of 18.8%."

**Original record:** Motion Picture Daily follow-up: the theatre owner confirms the test site and the paper restates the executives' account of no effect, Motion Picture Daily (unsigned), reporting B. S. Moss, president of the B. S. Moss Theatre Circuit, 1957-12-17. [Source ↗](https://archive.org/details/motionpicturedai82unse_0)

> Subliminal advertising equipment tested in the Fort Lee Theatre, Fort Lee, N. J., was that of the Subliminal Projection Company, New York, according to B. S. Moss, president of the B. S. Moss Theatre Circuit, owners and operators of the theatre. ... According to executives in immediate charge of theatre operation and advertising, the device tested had no effect on refreshment stand sales, and this was reported in the Motion Picture Daily story.

**Original record:** Same Motion Picture Daily follow-up: the owner's support rests only on figures from Vicary's company, Motion Picture Daily (unsigned), reporting B. S. Moss, 1957-12-17. [Source ↗](https://archive.org/details/motionpicturedai82unse_0)

> Moss said that on the basis of figures supplied him by the Subliminal Projection Company for the test period, he was satisfied that this type of "subconscious" advertising could help to increase sales. He said that the manager of the theatre and other members of his organization had not been informed of those figures.

**Evidence on the lesson:** Bermeitinger, Goelz, Johr, Neumann, Ecker and Doerr (2009), The hidden persuaders break into the tired brain, Journal of Experimental Social Psychology 45(2), 320-326 (accepted manuscript). [Source ↗](https://ssoar.info/ssoar/bitstream/document/29133/1/29133_1.pdf) · [Archived copy ↗](http://web.archive.org/web/20231203181842/https://www.ssoar.info/ssoar/handle/document/29133)

> We found the same pattern as Karremans et al. (2006): Only tired participants consumed more of the subliminally presented than the not presented brand. Therefore, the findings confirm that subjects are influenced by subliminally presented stimuli if these stimuli are need-related and if subjects are in the matching motivational state.

*Still uncertain:* A newspaper report made at the time, of what theatre executives said about their own sales, is treated here as a record of the event, not as relayed words; the same reading is used for the lemon-juice entry. The executives' account and the owner's account conflict, and no sales records from either side were found. Vicary's 1962 admission was not seen. If one reads the test itself as part of the story, the class is changed; if one reads the sales result as the story, it is invented.

### The Yale (or Harvard) class whose 3% with written goals later earned more than the other 97% together

**As usually told:** In 1953 (or 1979), researchers asked the graduating class at Yale (or Harvard) how many had clear, written goals. Only 3% did. Twenty years later, the 3% with written goals were worth more in money than the other 97% put together.

**The usual lesson:** Writing down specific goals makes people far more successful than people who do not.

**What happened:** No such study took place. The Yale University Library examined records and asked the secretary of the Class of 1953; it found no record, and nobody recalled such a study of that class or any other. The earliest telling found is Mark Victor Hansen's Future Diary (copyright 1983), which says the 1953 Yale class was polled, only three percent had written goals, and twenty years later those few had accomplished more than the other 97 percent. Hansen gives no source.

**Verdict:** Invented: it did not happen. The study itself: no Yale class was polled about written goals and followed up twenty years later. The Harvard versions are later retellings; the Matthews summary also calls the Yale/Harvard study an urban myth.

**Does the lesson hold?** Not established. The only direct test found (Matthews, Dominican University) had 149 participants who completed it, ran for 4 weeks, used self-rated progress and is not peer reviewed. It found that writing goals helped somewhat, which is far from the story's claim of larger lifetime earnings.

**Earliest telling found:** Future Diary (Newport Beach, Calif.: M.V. Hansen Pub. Co., copyright 1983; scan is a 1985 printing), item futurediary00hans, Mark Victor Hansen, 1983. [Source ↗](https://openlibrary.org/search/inside.json?q=%22class+was+polled%22&limit=100) · [Archived copy ↗](https://archive.org/details/futurediary00hans)

> University, in 1953, the graduating ... to see how many had written goals

**Original record:** Yale's own search of its records and of the Class of 1953, finding no study., Yale University Library (answered by Laura Galas), 2020-09-28 (last updated). [Source ↗](https://ask.library.yale.edu/faq/175224) · [Archived copy ↗](http://web.archive.org/web/20261012024028/https://ask.library.yale.edu/faq/175224)

**Evidence on the lesson:** Gail Matthews, 'Goals Research Summary', Dominican University of California. ### Nordstrom refunded a customer for tires, though it has never sold tires

**As usually told:** A customer rolled a set of car tyres into a Nordstrom store and asked for his money back. Nordstrom has never sold tyres, but the salesperson gave him a refund anyway. The story is told to show how far Nordstrom goes to keep a customer happy.

**The usual lesson:** Generous, no-questions-asked returns build customer loyalty that repays their cost.

**What happened:** In 1975 Nordstrom bought three Alaska stores from the Northern Commercial Company, and its Fairbanks store was in an old Northern Commercial building. The earliest telling found, in the Los Angeles Times of 15 December 1986, is a rule, not an event: Betsy Sanders, the Nordstrom vice president in charge of its Southern California stores, said staff were told to take back a tire if someone wheeled one in. From 2001 on, Nordstrom family members and the company said it really happened in Fairbanks in the mid- or late 1970s, and in 2015 a former clerk, Craig Trounce, was named as the salesperson. In that later account the customer had bought the tires from Northern Commercial in the same building, and the clerk paid him an estimated value. Every saved account that the event happened was published 26 or more years later and relays someone else's memory, and the accounts differ on the number of tires, the amount and the date.

**Verdict:** No record shows it happened, and none rules it out.

**Does the lesson hold?** Holds. A meta-analysis of 21 papers found that lenient return policies raise purchases more than they raise returns. It does not test refunds for goods a store never sold.

**Earliest telling found:** Not All Stores Will Cheerfully Give Refunds, S.J. Diamond, Los Angeles Times, quoting Betsy Sanders of Nordstrom, 1986-12-15. [Source ↗](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1986-12-15-fi-2829-story.html) · [Archived copy ↗](https://web.archive.org/web/20261122115330/https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1986-12-15-fi-2829-story.html)

### A ceramics class graded half on quantity and half on quality; the quantity group made the best pots

**As usually told:** A ceramics teacher split a class in two. One half was graded only on the quantity of pots it made, the other half only on the quality of one pot. At the end, the best pots all came from the quantity group, because they had practised while the quality group only theorised.

**The usual lesson:** Making a large quantity of work, and learning from each attempt, gives better quality than trying to make one perfect piece.

**What happened:** The story first appears, with no source and no names, in David Bayles and Ted Orland, Art & Fear (1993). In an email that James Clear quotes as dated 18 October 2016, Orland said the story came from a gambit that the photographer Jerry Uelsmann used with his Beginning Photography students at the University of Florida, 'as Jerry told it to me', and that Orland changed photography to ceramics. No statement by Orland under his own name was found that says this: not on his archived website (107 pages, 2001 to 2017), not in his 2006 book The View from the Studio Door, not in his own posts on The WELL (2006), and not in a 2003 interview. No record by Uelsmann of the grading experiment was found. Uelsmann did say in a 2013 interview that 'quantity produces quality', but he did not describe the two-group grading. So whether any class was graded this way, and what its results were, rests only on a story Uelsmann told Orland.

**Verdict:** No record shows it happened, and none rules it out. No record shows that any class was graded this way, or what its result was. Orland is quoted (in a 2016 email that James Clear published) as saying that the story he heard was about a photography class and that he changed it to ceramics. Orland witnessed only his own change of medium; the experiment itself rests on what Uelsmann told him. If the event took place, the field is wrong (photography, not ceramics), which is why misattributed is given as the second class.

**Does the lesson hold?** Not established. Studies of creative output support a weaker claim: people who produce more ideas also produce more good ideas (the equal-odds rule). No study found compares a group graded on quantity with a group graded on quality of one piece, as the story claims.

**Earliest telling found:** Art & Fear: Observations on the Perils (and Rewards) of Artmaking (text copyright 1993 by David Bayles and Ted Orland; the saved scan is an Image Continuum Press printing of 2010 or later), p. 29, David Bayles and Ted Orland, 1993. [Source ↗](https://artistryinaction.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/david_bayles_ted_orland_art_and_fear.pdf) · [Archived copy ↗](http://web.archive.org/web/20261103065732/https://artistryinaction.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/david_bayles_ted_orland_art_and_fear.pdf)

All those on the left side of the studio, he said, would be graded solely on the quantity of work they produced, all those on the right solely on its quality. ... the works of highest quality were all produced by the group being graded for quantity.

**Original record:** Uelsmann's own words on his teaching idea. It states the idea only. It does not record a class graded in two groups, or any result., Jerry Uelsmann (interviewed by Florida Trend), 2013-08-06.
