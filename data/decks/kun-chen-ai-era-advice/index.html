Kun Chen's advice for engineers in the AI era

Keep growing

  1. Test your growth every month, not every review

    • Ask what you can do this month that you could not do last month.
    • Waiting for a missed promotion to notice you have stalled is a lagging signal.
    • As tools change faster, shorten the interval further.

    Kun Chen "What did I do this month that I couldn't do last month?" (0:30, 2:00)

  2. When the work gets comfortable, move

    • Bored, or no longer learning: change team, company or role.
    • Each of his moves, Microsoft to Facebook, news to games, engineer to manager and back, followed curiosity.
    • Treat side quests as a signal that the main quest has gone stale.

    Kun Chen "Never get comfortable" with doing what you have always done. (55:01, 57:31)

Do more than you are assigned

  1. Get the fundamentals right, then take initiative

    • Being good at the assigned work is table stakes; doing it fast frees time.
    • Spend that time on problems other people have: no one asked him to build the Bing click-data tool.
    • If deadlines leave no slack, negotiate the expectations with your manager.

    Kun Chen "Take initiative and identify problems that other people have." (17:01, 18:01)

  2. Start scrappy, control scope, ship for feedback

    • Reuse existing building blocks instead of reinventing them.
    • Cut scope precisely so something useful reaches people quickly.
    • Show a first version to a few users and iterate on what they say.

    Kun Chen "Try not to reinvent the wheels." (15:01, 16:00)

  3. Talk to your users before you build

    • The Messenger games platform was designed around high scores and launched without asking developers.
    • Retention after the holidays was poor: turn-based and real-time games did not fit.
    • Asking game developers led to a redesign that gave them the flexibility.

    Kun Chen "We assumed it's going to work." (26:01, 27:00)

  4. The title does not decide your job

    • Do not hide in the engineer's corner, even as an introvert.
    • He travelled to game studios and shaped product decisions; that impact made staff.
    • Ask what the most impactful thing for the business is, whatever your title.

    Kun Chen "The title does not matter." (43:00)

  5. When things run smoothly, find the step change

    • If a project would run just as well without you, you are not adding value there.
    • Rethink the strategy rather than growing it incrementally.
    • Chaos is where the interesting problems are.

    Kun Chen "Taking initiative step function changes." (45:00, 45:31)

Advice by career stage

  1. Juniors: get very good, build a hundred things

    • Skill is the foundation of everything else.
    • He had built about a hundred projects before his first job; most earned nothing.
    • Side projects give breadth a big company's stable stack will not, and fun keeps you going.

    Kun Chen "Build a lot of them." (51:00, 52:00)

  2. Seniors: know your leverage when you move

    • At senior levels, former colleagues recruit you; that is leverage.
    • The default is a lateral move, because the hiring company has every reason to offer one.
    • One negotiation can be worth a level, often one you already earned.

    Kun Chen "Realizing your leverage and know to negotiate." (58:30, 59:32)

  3. Management is support, not bigger scope

    • Wanting bigger impact or scope is a reason to stay an IC, not to manage.
    • Managing is supporting a team, recruiting and growing people.
    • Going back to IC on the same team worked; no one is watching as closely as you think.

    Kun Chen "Management is really more about supporting others." (30:31, 31:01, 39:01)

Working with AI

  1. The 2% are pulling ahead; act with urgency

    • CTOs he spoke with see roughly 10 to 15% company-wide gains from AI.
    • About 2% of engineers use it deeply and are far more productive.
    • Impactful projects, and tokens, are moving to those people.

    Kun Chen "An industrial revolution happening right in front of our eyes." (1:02:00, 1:03:01, 1:04:02)

  2. Treat every tool as temporary scaffold

    • The tooling will keep changing until the models plateau.
    • Expect to rebuild your setup every six months or sooner.
    • Invest in a habit of continuous learning, not in one specific tool.

    Kun Chen "Assuming everything is just temporary scaffold." (1:08:01, 1:09:02)

  3. Juniors can leapfrog; seniors orchestrate

    • Mastery of one language or framework is worth less now, so the gap to seniors has narrowed.
    • Seniors still hold scarce skills: architecture, scaling, flaky production.
    • They apply those by supervising many agents, not by coding everything themselves.

    Kun Chen "A really good opportunity to leapfrog." (1:11:01, 1:13:32, 1:16:01)

  4. Automate your review judgement

    • A senior who reviews every AI change will lose that battle.
    • Turn what you look for in review into a skill or an agent.
    • Ask whether PR review, built for one human checking another, should still exist.

    Kun Chen "Not carry the burden manually." (1:20:00, 1:21:30, 1:22:00)

  5. Learn to lose control

    • Micromanaging agents' code makes you the bottleneck.
    • Write down principles that steer all the agents, as a manager would.
    • Move up a level: ask about testing, dogfooding and load, as an engineering manager does, without reading every line.

    Kun Chen "Learning to lose control is actually a very important skill." (34:30, 1:24:30, 1:25:31)

  6. Takeaway

    • Keep the monthly test, and move when you stop learning.
    • Do the unassigned work: talk to users, ship small, chase the step change.
    • With AI, invest in mindset, orchestration and judgement over any one tool.

    Focus on the right problems, and bring judgement and taste to them. (1:33:02)
