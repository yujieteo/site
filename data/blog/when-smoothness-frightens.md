I watched [Kun Chen's interview on A Life Engineered](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q3ca41YIZjc),
"The 2% of Engineers Winning the AI Era", in which the host, Steve, walks Kun
through a career from Microsoft intern to E7 at Facebook, partner level back at
Microsoft, and now solo builder. Most of it is good career and AI advice. One
passage is something else, and it is the part I keep returning to.

## The passage

Steve asks how Kun had the nose to go after a bigger scope than turn-based games
in Messenger ([44:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q3ca41YIZjc&t=2698s)).
Kun says that whenever a project was running well and everything was going
smoothly, "I would just panic". He would ask what was wrong. His reason
([45:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q3ca41YIZjc&t=2731s)): if the project
would keep going whether he was there or not, he was not adding value, so why
was he there at all?

Steve names how unusual this is
([46:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q3ca41YIZjc&t=2762s)). Most people in
chaos work hard so that one day they reach smooth seas where things run
themselves. Kun is built the other way round: the smooth sea is what produces
the anxiety. Kun agrees. Chaos is the fun part, something to understand and
solve; when things go well he has, in his words, a crisis of "why am I here"
([47:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q3ca41YIZjc&t=2824s)), and goes
looking for something more chaotic. That is why he left Facebook games once the
platform was established, to build one from zero at Microsoft.

The same shape appears ten minutes earlier, when he explains leaving management
([36:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q3ca41YIZjc&t=2175s)). Offered a
larger organisation, he suddenly felt he could see the next ten years, and a
predictable decade was not a state he wanted to be in. He says something in his
subconscious told him not to go deeper.

## A psychoanalytic reading

What follows is my interpretation, not anything Kun claims.

The ordinary reading is that he is ambitious and easily bored. But boredom is
not what he describes. He describes panic, and the question that comes with it
is not "what next?" but "why am I here?". The fear is not that the project will
fail. It is that the project no longer needs him.

Lacan's remark on anxiety fits this closely. Anxiety, he says, is not the
response to a lack; it arises when the lack is lacking, when there is no gap
left in which one is needed. A project in chaos is full of gaps, and each one
addresses him: understand this, fix this. A project that runs itself has closed
every gap, and the question that the work had been answering for him, what am
I for, comes back unanswered. Chaos is not only fun; it is where he is wanted.

The career then reads as a repetition. Microsoft to Facebook, news to games,
engineer to manager and back, Facebook to Microsoft, big tech to solo: at each
point where things settle, he leaves for a place where the lack is restored.
Kun tells this as following curiosity, and I believe him; curiosity and
repetition are not exclusive. He is also unusually candid that part of it is
not chosen, that something below his reasons says no.

None of this is a diagnosis, and it does not make the pattern a problem. It has
served him well, by his own account and by his levels. I find it useful because
it separates two things I would otherwise run together: wanting to learn, and
needing to be needed.

## Out of the loop

The passage is sharper next to the rest of the interview, which is largely
about removing yourself. Kun says his time as a manager taught him to "lose
control" ([34:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q3ca41YIZjc&t=2052s)), and
that the same lesson applies to agents: stop micromanaging the code they write
and set principles instead. Later he imagines the human moving up the hierarchy
as the models improve, from senior engineer to manager to director to CEO of an
organisation of agents
([1:23:29](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q3ca41YIZjc&t=5009s)), and asks
whether pull request review should exist at all when the author is already the
check ([1:19:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q3ca41YIZjc&t=4794s)).

That is a description of a system that keeps going whether you are there or
not: exactly the state he says frightens him. I noted from an earlier
interview of his that the aim is to move ourselves out of the loop as much as
possible ([notes, 28 September](../notes.html#2026-09-28)), and I am building
towards the same thing with my own crewmates, which already land work before I
answer about it. If the smooth sea is the goal of agent work, the person who
dreads it has two options: keep leaving for fresh chaos, or find a version of
being needed that survives the work running without them. Kun's answer, I
think, is the first, one level up each time. Each rung of his hierarchy is a
new place where the system has gaps and someone has to answer for them.

I do not know which answer is right for me. But the question "why am I here?"
is worth asking before the system makes it urgent, rather than after.

## Other points worth keeping

- **The monthly test** ([0:33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q3ca41YIZjc&t=33s)):
  what did I do this month that I could not do last month? A missed promotion is
  a lagging indicator of not growing.
- **The 2%** ([1:02:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q3ca41YIZjc&t=3720s)):
  by his account of conversations with CTOs, a company-wide gain of 10 to 15%
  from AI hides about 2% of engineers who use it deeply.
- **Tooling is scaffold** ([1:08:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q3ca41YIZjc&t=4088s)):
  expect to rebuild your agent setup every six months until the models plateau,
  and invest in the habit of learning rather than in one tool.
- **Side quests** ([57:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q3ca41YIZjc&t=3440s)):
  catching yourself on a side quest is a sign the main quest has stopped being
  interesting.
