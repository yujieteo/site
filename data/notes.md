A beam is statically indeterminate when its supports provide more reaction components than equilibrium can find. A beam in a plane has three independent equilibrium equations, one for forces along it, one for forces across it, and one for moments, so the degree of indeterminacy of a single beam is the number of reactions minus three; R. C. Hibbeler's *Structural Analysis* counts determinacy the same way. A simply supported beam, with a pin and a roller, has three reactions and is determinate. A propped cantilever, fixed at one end and resting on a roller at the other, has four, so it is indeterminate to the first degree, and so is a beam continuous over three supports. A beam fixed at both ends has six reactions; with no axial load its two horizontal reactions drop out along with the horizontal equation, so for bending it has two redundants. Each redundant needs one extra equation, and that equation comes from compatibility: a deflection or a slope that the real supports force to take a known value, usually zero. #structural-engineering

The force method, also called the flexibility or compatibility method, solves an indeterminate beam in five steps. Count the redundants and choose one, for example the force in a prop. Remove it, which leaves a determinate primary structure such as a cantilever. Find the deflection at the released support caused by the loads, and the deflection there per unit of the redundant force. Write compatibility: the real support does not move, so the load deflection minus the redundant times its unit deflection equals zero, which gives the redundant. Then equilibrium gives every other reaction. The choice of redundant is free; releasing the wall moment of a propped cantilever instead leaves a simply supported beam and gives the same answer. The deflection formulas it needs are those of determinate beams, tabulated for example in Gere and Goodno's *Mechanics of Materials*. #structural-engineering

Worked example of the force method: a propped cantilever six metres long, fixed at the wall and propped at the free end, carries a uniform load of ten kilonewtons per metre. Released at the prop, it is a cantilever, whose tip sags w L to the fourth over eight E I under the load; with E I of sixteen thousand kilonewton square metres (steel at 200 gigapascals and a second moment of area of 8 times ten to the minus five metres to the fourth) that is 101.25 millimetres. A unit upward tip force lifts the tip L cubed over three E I. Setting the two equal gives the prop force as three eighths of the total load, three w L over eight, which is 22.5 kilonewtons; E I cancels, so the reactions of a uniform beam do not depend on its stiffness. Equilibrium then gives 37.5 kilonewtons at the wall and a hogging wall moment of w L squared over eight, 45 kilonewton metres. The site's [beam diagram creator](visuals/beamdiag/index.html) and its exact-arithmetic reference solver give the same values. #structural-engineering

In that propped cantilever, the prop cuts the largest bending moment from 180 kilonewton metres to 45. Without the prop the wall carries w L squared over two, 180 kilonewton metres. With it, the moment at the wall is 45 kilonewton metres hogging, the moment is zero at a quarter of the span from the wall, 1.5 metres, and the largest sagging moment is nine w L squared over 128, 25.3125 kilonewton metres, where the shear is zero, 3.75 metres from the wall. The point where the moment changes sign, the point of contraflexure, is where tension moves from the top of the beam to the bottom. #structural-engineering

For a beam continuous over several supports, the convenient redundants are the bending moments over the interior supports. Cutting the beam at those supports turns each span into a simply supported beam and breaks the continuity of slope; restoring it at each interior support gives Clapeyron's three-moment equation of 1857, derived for example in Megson's *Structural and Stress Analysis*. For a uniform E I, supports at one level, and a uniform load on each span, it reads: the moment at the left support times the left span, plus twice the middle support moment times the sum of the two spans, plus the moment at the right support times the right span, equals minus w one L one cubed over four, minus w two L two cubed over four. Each right-hand term is six E I times the end slope of a simply supported span, w L cubed over twenty-four E I. At pinned ends the moment is zero, so one interior support gives one equation. #structural-engineering

Worked example of the three-moment equation: a beam continuous over two spans of four and six metres, pinned at the far ends, carries ten kilonewtons per metre along its whole length. With zero moments at the ends, twice the support moment times ten metres equals minus 160 minus 540, so twenty times the support moment is minus 700 and the moment over the middle support is 35 kilonewton metres, hogging. Taking each span as a free body with that end moment gives the reactions: 11.25 kilonewtons at the end of the short span, 24.17 at the end of the long span, and 28.75 plus 35.83, or 64.58 kilonewtons, at the middle support, which carries well over half of the 100 kilonewton load. The largest sagging moments are 6.33 kilonewton metres in the short span, 1.125 metres from its end, and 29.2 in the long span, 2.42 metres from its end; the short span hogs over its last 1.75 metres, pulled up over the support by the long span's load. #structural-engineering

Moment distribution, published by Hardy Cross in 1930, reaches the same support moment by a different route, using stiffness rather than flexibility. For the two-span beam of four and six metres, lock the middle joint against rotation; with the far ends pinned, each span starts with a fixed-end moment of w L squared over eight, 20 kilonewton metres for the short span and 45 for the long one, acting in opposite senses. Release the joint: the unbalance of 25 kilonewton metres is shared in proportion to each member's stiffness, three E I over L for a member whose far end is pinned, which gives shares of 0.6 and 0.4, so 15 goes to the short span and 10 to the long one. Both end moments come out at 35 kilonewton metres, the same answer as the three-moment equation. With one joint and pinned far ends nothing carries over, so a single balance is exact. #structural-engineering

Four checks catch most mistakes in an indeterminate beam. First, equilibrium: in the two-span example the reactions 11.25, 64.58 and 24.17 kilonewtons add up to the 100 kilonewton load, and moments about any point balance. Equilibrium alone cannot confirm the answer, though, because any wrong value of the redundant still balances once the other reactions follow from it. Second, a limit case: with equal spans the three-moment equation must give a support moment of w L squared over eight, 45 kilonewton metres for two six-metre spans. Third, compatibility: the deflection must come out zero at every support. Fourth, an independent method: moment distribution, or the stiffness-method [beam diagram creator](visuals/beamdiag/index.html) on this site, must agree. Both worked examples here pass all four. #structural-engineering

A metagame shifts when the shared perception of what is strong changes, and a tournament win is a common trigger. A core is the synergy a team is built around, but a mode is a smaller package that works together while sitting apart from the rest of the team. Context engineering is the art and science of curating what information goes into an LLM's limited attention window from a constantly evolving universe of possible data.
