Information gain

What should you check next?

Start with what you believe, compare what you could learn, and see which observation reduces uncertainty fastest.

What is information gain?

Before you check something, you are uncertain whether a hypothesis H is true. A check is worth making when its possible results would move your belief. Expected information gain measures, in bits, how much uncertainty a check is expected to remove: the uncertainty now minus the uncertainty you expect to have after seeing its result.

Why is 50% maximally uncertain?

For a yes-or-no question, believing 50% means you have no lean either way: 1 bit of uncertainty. At 25% or 75% there is about 0.81 bits left, at 10% or 90% about 0.47 bits, and at 0% or 100% none. 10% and 90% hold the same uncertainty, just leaning opposite ways.

Why must a useful check behave differently under H and not H?

A result that is just as likely whether H is true or false cannot tell the two apart, however dramatic it sounds. A useful check is one whose results depend strongly on which possibility is true.

Worked example

Prior: 50%.
Check: P(E | H) = 90%, P(E | not H) = 10%.
If E occurs: posterior = 90%.
If E does not occur: posterior = 10%.

Either result moves you a long way from 50%, because E is nine times more likely if H is true than if it is false, and its absence nine times more likely if H is false. Uncertainty drops from 1 bit to about 0.47 bits whichever result you see, so the check is expected to teach about 0.53 bits.

      Why does this work?

      A useful observation is one whose possible results depend strongly on whether H or not H is true. Not one that sounds dramatic, and not simply one that is likely.

      A bit has no everyday physical meaning here. The useful reading is: more bits remaining means more uncertainty still unresolved. 50% is maximum uncertainty for a yes-or-no question; at exactly 0% or 100% there is none left, and ordinary updating can no longer move the belief.

      Expected and actual learning differ. Before checking, expected learning averages over the results you might see. After checking, the belief change from the particular result can be much larger or smaller: a check that usually teaches little can occasionally produce a very informative result.

      Three quantities, plus surprise.

      1. Remaining uncertainty: Shannon entropy of your belief.
      2. Expected learning before checking: expected information gain, equal to the mutual information between the hypothesis and the result.
      3. Belief change after a result: the KL divergence from your prior to your posterior.
      4. Surprise of the observed result: −log₂ P(result). Related to, but not the same as, belief change.
      h(p) = −p log₂ p − (1−p) log₂(1−p),  h(0) = h(1) = 0
P(E) = a p + b (1−p)          a = P(E | H), b = P(E | not H)
P(H | E) = a p / P(E)
P(H | not E) = (1−a) p / [(1−a) p + (1−b)(1−p)]
H_after = P(E) h(P(H|E)) + P(not E) h(P(H|not E))
IG = h(p) − H_after = I(H; E) = Σ_e P(e) D_KL(P(H|e) ‖ P(H))
D_KL(q ‖ p) = q log₂(q/p) + (1−q) log₂((1−q)/(1−p))
surprise(e) = −log₂ P(e)
rate = IG / time  (time in minutes; zero time is "essentially free")
