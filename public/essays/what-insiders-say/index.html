What insiders say

24 October 2026

In 2024 I read a lot of r/AskReddit threads where people tell the secrets of their own industry. Each time an answer surprised me, I took a screenshot. By the end of the year I had about two hundred of them.

In 2022 I wrote that I wished for a source that gave an insider view. I did not expect to find one in Reddit comments. But there it was, one trade at a time, from the people who do the work.

A warning first. Everything below is a claim by a stranger who says they work in a trade. I cannot check most of it, and some of it is surely wrong. The replies often disagreed, and the replies were often the best part. So read each one as "an insider wrote that", not as fact. Here are the claims that surprised me most.

#Money traps

The one I remember best is about insurance. An insurance worker wrote that if you call your insurer only to ask for an estimate, the call can go on your file as a claim of zero dollars. Other insurers see that file later, so a question can raise your price or stop you from getting a new policy. Their advice was to ask an independent inspector first. Two other people wrote that this happened to them, one after a cracked windscreen and one after a broken door handle. I had always thought that a question was free.

Another comment was one line long. A bill every 28 days is 13 bills a year, not 12. I had to do the sum before I believed it: 13 times 28 is 364.

Someone wrote that the Black Friday doorbuster TVs are not the normal stock at a lower price. They come in a separate shipment, with different model numbers, and they cost less to make. By their sums, you pay 300 dollars for a TV worth 200, not for a TV worth 1000 at a discount. And someone else wrote that a lot of expensive air conditioner repairs are only a new capacitor of about 10 dollars. A reply said that a technician quoted 500 dollars, and the capacitor cost 12.

#Same product, different label

A person who worked on factory automation wrote that at a big bread bakery, the brand bread and the store-brand bread were the same. When the brand run ended, a new roll of bags went on, and the same batch went into the bags of the next store brand. They said that an orange juice plant was the same: one set of lines, a dozen brands. A toilet paper factory worker wrote that the cheap and the name brands use the same paper on the same machines, and only the pattern pressed into the paper changes.

A wine bottling worker wrote that one winery put the same wine, from the same tank, into nicer bottles at up to three times the price. A salesperson wrote that a milk plant put its milk into organic and normal cartons. All of it was organic, but the plant sold some as normal milk at a lower price, so that it did not lose those buyers. A reply went further. It said that the cheap option is sometimes made worse on purpose, so that some people pay double for the better one.

A dentist wrote that if a toothpaste has fluoride, it is basically the same as the others. The exception is toothpaste for sensitive teeth, which has an extra active ingredient. And a comment about pool chemicals said that "alkalinity increaser" is only baking soda.

#Marketing words that mean little

"Military grade" was the biggest surprise. I thought it meant tough. One person wrote that it means the lowest bidder that can appear to pass the requirements.

"Genuine leather" started an argument. One person wrote that it is the lowest grade of leather, and that you want "full grain" or "top grain". Another replied that it is not a grade at all. It only says that the thing is leather, and it tells you nothing about quality. The same thread said that "handmade" only means that a person in the factory touched the item.

A factory worker wrote that one of their jobs was to add one small part to a kit from abroad and put a "Made in USA" label on the box. A reply said that this is illegal, and that "Assembled in USA" exists for this case. A jeweller wrote that a watch can say "Swiss made" with only a set share of Swiss work.

The small print on the bottle was the worst. A person who made shower gel at a contract factory wrote that a batch of 40,000 pounds got about 2 ounces of vitamin E, the least amount that let the label name it. A head of production for luxury fashion brands wrote that a jacket made from recycled plastic bottles cannot be recycled again, while the bottles could have become new bottles. In their view, the "sustainable" jacket is where the loop ends. One claim went the other way. Someone from the perfume trade wrote that synthetic ingredients are often the safer ones, because the parts that cause allergies are removed.

Then there are the words "sold out". Someone who works with concert tickets wrote that 99 percent of sold-out concerts are not really sold out, because the organisers hold tickets back and release them near the show. Also, at the start of a sale, bots put a lot of tickets in their carts and drop them after about nine minutes. Their advice was to wait ten minutes. And someone wrote that a group of authors can each pay about 200 dollars into one anthology, set its price to 99 cents and use the money to buy copies for their readers. The book reaches the top of a bestseller list, and every author in it is now a bestselling author.

#Hygiene

Ice was the big surprise. A cafe worker wrote that no cafe they worked at ever trained staff to clean the ice machine, so it was never cleaned. An inspector wrote that when they unscrewed the nozzle of a soda fountain at a gas station, a string of mould fell out, almost as long as their forearm. My favourite reply was about a fast food place with the best soda. Someone asked the manager for the secret. The answer was that they followed the cleaning schedule of the maker.

Gloves went the other way. A food worker wrote that a cook with bare hands is probably cleaner than a cook with gloves. People with gloves change them less often, and some take money with their gloves on and then make the next sandwich. The cook with bare hands almost always washes first.

Someone wrote that most hotels wash the sheets, but often only shake out the blankets between guests. Someone else wrote that US rules make airlines clean the drinking water tanks of a plane only four times a year. The replies asked how often the reader cleans the hot water tank at home.

#Protecting the elderly

This group was not funny. One comment said that people "befriend" lonely old homeowners, pay the property tax on the house for them, and in the end take the house instead of the heirs, for much less than it is worth, and within the law. The writer said that they find the victims in property databases that show whose house is paid off. The heirs often live far away, and they find out too late. If even part of this is true, it is a reason to stay close to old relatives and to know who pays their bills.

A person who worked in two care homes wrote that the homes saved money on everything. Food came off the trucks rotten, and staff were told to pick out the bad parts and serve the rest. A room for one person cost 2500 to 3000 dollars a month, and the residents could spend only 150 to 200 dollars of their own money each month. A caregiver of 20 wrote that some old clients live in houses with cockroaches, and that the families visit but do not look.

#Things that are always broken

The last group surprised me in a better way. Someone wrote that the internet is always broken somewhere, and that it works only because everything in it has massive redundancy. An airline pilot wrote that there is probably something broken on the plane you fly on, but never anything that matters for safety. A minimum equipment list says what must work. In another thread someone wrote that pilots make mistakes all the time, and a reply explained the Swiss cheese model. Each layer of safety has holes, and an accident needs the holes in many layers to line up.

An engineer wrote that building codes accept a chance of about 1 percent, in theory, that the loads on a building are more than its strength. They said that the true chance is probably less than 0.1 percent, because engineers leave some sources of strength out of the sums. And "failure" in the code means cracks or bending, not collapse.

A nurse wrote that doctors often look up a procedure just before they do it. An IT worker wrote that they often find the fix for your problem with a search. At first this worried me. Then it made sense. As one comment said, no one really knows everything about what they do. The internet, the plane and the building do not need anyone to. They need layers of checks, so that one mistake does not become a disaster.

That is what I took from two hundred screenshots. Most of the secrets were not dark. They were about money and about who checks: a batch in a new bag, a word on a label, an ice machine that nobody cleans because nobody looks. Now when I read a claim like these, I ask who is paid to look. And I read the replies.

The claims are paraphrased from r/AskReddit threads where people tell the secrets of their industry. I read them in 2024. The screenshots are gone, so I give no links.
