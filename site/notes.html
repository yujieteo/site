A metagame shifts when the shared perception of what is strong changes, and a tournament win is a common trigger. Wolfe Glick's Metagame page gives the worked case: after he won the Players Cup 2 with Coalossal, Coalossal's usage rose, Pokémon that matched up well into it rose with it, and "it became much more challenging to win with Coalossal." The same mechanism explains why a streamer's or champion's team often performs worse for someone who copies it: the niche that team exploited may already be crowded out by the counters its own success invited. Practical use: when you adopt a successful team, ask which opposing teams it was built to beat and whether those are still the popular ones, and expect your own standout result to draw the same response; read popularity as a moving matchup problem rather than a fixed list of strong Pokémon.

In a best-of-three, game 1 is a chance to learn as well as win. Aaron Zheng's Team Preview guide frames the between-game decisions as questions about leads, Pokémon selection, and information: how the previous lead match-up went, whether the opponent can counter your lead in game 2, which of your Pokémon actually contributed, and what the opponent revealed. The guide's worked example is that once an opposing Zacian shows Behemoth Blade, Sacred Sword, Quick Attack, and Protect, you know it has no Play Rough and therefore applies much less pressure to a Palkia. Practical use: after each game, record the opponent's revealed moves, items, and modes, then pick the four that exploit the now-known set; keep the game-1 lead informative enough to gain that knowledge without conceding the set, and prefer adjustments that stay strong against more than one of the opponent's likely leads, since they are adjusting too.

A core is the synergy a team is built around, but a mode is a smaller package that works together while sitting apart from the rest of the team. VGC guide's Cores and Modes (Wolfe Glick) separates teambuilding modes — two or three Pokémon such as Politoed and Kingdra for rain, or Indeedee-F and Hatterene as a Trick Room mode on an otherwise fast team — from battling modes, the reusable game plans you actually bring, like leading Xerneas and Rillaboom with Incineroar and Amoonguss in the back. The trade is flexibility for depth: a Pokémon that only functions inside its mode, such as Escavalier under Trick Room, or a pair that must be led together, must always be brought with its partner, which shrinks your lead and back-line choices and can telegraph every viable lead during team preview. Treat a mode as a deliberate purchase: count how many lead and back combinations it leaves, and prefer modes whose pieces still do something outside the package; a team with a core and no mode is flexible but shallow, while a team whose only plan is one mode is easy to read.

Aaron Zheng's first step in team preview is to read the opponent's six for combinations that can win on turn 1: ability combos (Charizard and Groudon, Weezing and Regigigas), Fake Out plus a strong attacker (Incineroar and Zacian), speed control plus an attacker (Whimsicott and Kyogre), redirection plus setup (Amoonguss and Dusclops for Trick Room), and Weakness Policy enablers. He then checks for a single Pokémon with "1v4" potential, such as Shedinja or an Iron Defense Ferrothorn, because one unanswerable Pokémon can beat the four you brought. The Team Preview guide's point is that these combinations are the most likely leads, so naming them is how you get ahead of turn 1. Practical use: name the opponent's two or three most likely leads and the one Pokémon you must always be able to answer, then confirm your four cover them; when they do not, choose the lead and back line that minimize what their best combo takes from you.

Some cores recur across formats because their typings cover each other's weaknesses. VGC guide's Cores and Modes (Wolfe Glick) follows Fire/Water/Grass, where each type resists what threatens the others, through VGC17's Arcanine, Tapu Fini, and Kartana — a trio whose complementary stats and Speed bands also suited bulky offense built on careful switching — and Dragon/Fairy/Steel, where Aegislash and Hydreigon resist each other's Ground, Fire, Ghost, and Dark and Fighting, Dragon, Fairy, and Ice weaknesses, with Mega Mawile as a third piece. Glick is explicit that a core is not only typing: ability, base stats, and Speed all contribute, and a classic triangle is weaker outside the format that made it. The part that transfers is the defensive coverage. Practical use: before inventing a pairing for a centerpiece, check whether an existing typing triangle already answers its weaknesses, and judge the core by how its members switch into each other's threats rather than by combined offense alone.

Expected goal involvement (xGI, expected goals plus expected assists) is a better guide to future returns than points scored, because finishing over a short window is noisy. After Gameweek 5 of 2026/27, Pascal Groß (Brighton) has seven goal involvements on 2.63 xGI, a surplus of +4.37, and João Pedro (Chelsea) has six on 2.36 (+3.64), while Igor Thiago (Brentford) has a single goal involvement on 3.29 xGI and Thierno Barry (Everton) has two on 3.65; the figures come from the per-player expected_goal_involvements, expected_goals, and expected_assists fields in bootstrap-static. Read xGI as the regression baseline: a player far above it is a sell-high or avoid candidate unless something structural changed, such as a new role, penalty duty, or a stronger side, and a player far below it is a buy-low candidate whose price and ownership have not caught up. The caveat is that five gameweeks is a small sample and xGI is itself an estimate, so treat the gap as a prompt to check minutes, role, and the next fixtures rather than as a forecast.

Goalkeeper value is unusually flat, so paying a premium for a famous keeper rarely buys points. After Gameweek 5 of 2026/27 the highest-scoring keeper is Tzolakis (Hull City) with 34 points at £4.7m, ahead of Raya (Arsenal) with 30 at £6.1m and Alisson (Liverpool) with 27 at £5.5m, and among keepers with at least 400 minutes Tzolakis leads on points per million (7.2) while Raya and Alisson sit near 4.9. The mechanism is visible in bootstrap-static: Tzolakis has made 17 saves to Raya's 9 because Hull face more shots, and saves score one point per three, so a busy keeper on a mid-table side can match a clean-sheet keeper on a strong one, and Hull have also kept three clean sheets. The practical read is to spend the minimum that buys a guaranteed starter and put the saved budget into outfield positions, while checking that the cheap keeper's team is not so poor that clean sheets and save volume both dry up.

Expected goals separate a team's underlying strength from its results, which is where buys and sells come from. The practical use is to prefer attacking assets from sides whose expected goals are high but whose results lag, and to treat a team scoring well above expectation as a candidate for regression, while confirming the numbers over more than five matches before acting.

The ep_next field in FPL's bootstrap-static is not an independent forecast of the next fixture: after Gameweek 5 of 2026/27 it equals form for every leading player (Groß 11.2, Tarkowski 9.2, Schade 9.0, Bogle 9.0, Haaland 8.0). Treating ep_next as a model output overstates its value; the more informative fields are expected goal involvement, minutes played, and the next fixtures.

Agent Skills package procedural knowledge as a directory containing a SKILL.md file so a general-purpose agent can load domain expertise on demand. Anthropic's Agent Skills guidance describes three levels of progressive disclosure: the name and description of every installed skill preloaded into the system prompt, the full SKILL.md read only once a skill looks relevant, and bundled reference files or scripts read only when a specific step needs them, which keeps the context cost of a skill proportional to the task. Deterministic work belongs in bundled code the agent executes without loading into context, since sorting or parsing through token generation is slower and less repeatable than running a script. Skills should be built from observed gaps rather than anticipated ones, and installed only from trusted sources because a skill's instructions and code act with the agent's own tools.

Loading every tool definition upfront and passing each intermediate result back through the model is what makes large MCP tool sets expensive, because a long document can cross the context window twice. Anthropic's code execution with MCP instead generates a file tree of tools so the agent reads only the definitions a task needs, cutting a worked example from roughly 150,000 tokens to 2,000, and lets results be filtered inside the execution environment before returning them. The trade is that tool discovery becomes filesystem navigation, so directory naming and a searchable tool index now carry the retrieval burden that a flat tool list used to carry.

Traditional retrieval loses the context that makes a chunk findable. Anthropic's contextual retrieval prepends a short chunk-specific explanation, generated from the whole document, before embedding the chunk and before building the BM25 index, reporting 49% fewer failed retrievals and 67% fewer when reranking is added. Pairing the two methods matters because embeddings miss exact identifiers such as an error code while lexical BM25 matches them, so fusing semantic and exact-match rankings covers both kinds of query. The rule generalizes past RAG: a fragment's meaning, and therefore its retrievability, depends on context that chunking stripped away, which is why each note here stays self-contained.

Separate workflows from agents before building either. Anthropic's building effective agents defines workflows as LLMs and tools orchestrated through predefined code paths, while agents direct their own process and tool use, and it recommends finding the simplest solution first because agentic systems trade latency and cost for task performance that only flexible, model-driven decisions can justify. The reusable building blocks are an augmented LLM with retrieval, tools, and memory, then the workflow patterns of prompt chaining, routing, and parallelization, each of which should be adopted only when the task decomposes cleanly enough to benefit. Frameworks hide the prompts and responses they abstract, so starting with direct API calls keeps the failure modes legible.

The metagame is a description of what opponents are likely to bring, not a manual for what you must play. VGC guide's Metagame (Wolfe Glick) defines it as the popular Pokémon, combinations, and teams in your specific context — ladder, local, or a friend's group — and notes it can shift after a single strong performance, as when a tournament win raised Coalossal's usage and dragged its counters up with it. Treating it as a threat list, rather than a shopping list, is what earns the edge: you can bring something unpopular as long as you have already answered the popular threats and are not caught off-guard by a shift.

A core is a relationship between jobs, not a fixed pair of species: VGC guide's Cores and Modes treats cores as the recurring jobs Pokémon do for each other, and modes as the coherent ways a team shifts into a plan, so the same core can be filled by different bodies. Reg M-C's top pairings fit this reading — Fake Out plus Intimidate glue (Rillaboom, Incineroar) enabling an offensive Mega — as a relationship that could be rebuilt with substitutes. Building around roles keeps a team adjustable: when a partner stops fitting, you can swap the body while preserving the job, and you can recognize the same core in an opponent's team even when the species differ.

In Pokémon Champions' VGC 2026 Regulation Set M-C, Mega Evolutions change what a team must answer, not just what it can use: Pikalytics' Reg M-C data is crowded with Mega forms (Salamence-Mega, Raichu-Mega-Y, Floette-Eternal-Mega, Charizard-Mega-Y, Swampert-Mega, Metagross-Mega, Froslass-Mega, Dragonite-Mega, Staraptor-Mega), while each of the highest-placed tournament teams builds exactly one Mega into a cohesive six. The practical consequence is that "can I beat their Mega?" is a team-preview question before it is a battle question, because a single Mega both concentrates a team's offense and hands the opponent a fixed target to prepare for.

Because both players commit to a turn at the same time, the useful pre-move question is not "what will they do" but "what does each Pokémon threaten". VGC guide's Pressure (Aaron Traylor) defines pressure as the threat of proactive action: proactive moves advance your goal directly (damage, knockouts, speed control, status) while reactive moves (switching, Protect, healing) exist only to answer a specific threat, and predicting means committing to a line based on how your opponent will react to that pressure. In practice, before choosing, name which side holds pressure, then either take the lowest-risk proactive line or identify the exact threat you must answer; this turns a vague guess about the opponent into a decision about options you can actually trade.

How you build depends on how consolidated the format is. VGC guide's Archetypes (Aaron Traylor) defines an archetype as a core that has become popular enough that players iterate on it and optimize its partners, and it frames the format as a spectrum: in a centralized metagame there are few archetypes but they are well-optimized and hard to beat, while a decentralized metagame leaves more room for new directions. Reg M-C's clustered cores around Rillaboom, Incineroar, and the Mega forms read as a consolidating format, so a new team should start by naming the current archetypes and checking whether its answer to each is a real line or just a hope.

Rillaboom and Incineroar recur across Reg M-C pairings — Rillaboom+Sneasler (24.7%), Incineroar+Rillaboom (23.7%), and Rillaboom+Salamence-Mega (23.3%) lead the two-Pokémon cores — which fits them as a backbone rather than an archetype: Fake Out and Intimidate buy the tempo an offensive partner needs, and VGC guide's Cores and Modes treats such glue as the relational layer that many teams share. The teambuilding consequence is to decide early which Mega your glue exists to support, then plan answers for the popular partners the opponent's glue will bring.

Speed control wins games by denying the opponent their turn, not by moving first for its own sake. VGC guide's Speed Control (Wolfe Glick) separates the modes and recommends at least two per team: Tailwind (fast or priority setters, offensive partners, struggles against Trick Room), Trick Room (slowest-first, bulky setter plus sweeper, always resolves last at -7 priority), speed drops such as Icy Wind and Electroweb (low commitment, reset when the target switches), and speed boosts (survive knockouts, but limited distribution). The practical rule is to commit to a mode during team preview, because Tailwind and Trick Room are near-perfect counters to each other, and the setter you expose is the line your opponent will target.

Team preview matters more in VGC than in 6v6 because you must also choose which two of your six to leave out. VGC guide's Team Preview (Aaron Zheng) frames it as three goals: find a lead that sets the pace, decide the two in the back, and confirm the four you bring can answer every Pokémon on the opponent's six. Working backwards — eliminating Pokémon that contribute little on offense, defense, or as a combo piece, and making sure you cover any single Pokémon with "1v4" potential — turns a favorites list into an answers check. In practice, name each opponent's obvious combo and speed profile during preview, then verify your four have a line to every member before you lock in.

Usage and tournament performance answer different questions. Pikalytics' Reg M-C page ranks the most-used Pokémon and the most common pairings (Rillaboom+Sneasler on 2,171 teams at 24.7%, Incineroar+Rillaboom on 2,083 at 23.7%, Rillaboom+Salamence-Mega on 2,049 at 23.3%), while its top-performing teams are scattered outliers: a 12-0 Floette-Eternal-Mega squad sits beside a 10-1 Swampert-Mega rain team and an 8-0 Charizard-Mega-Y sun team. A high usage share estimates how often you will face a Pokémon and tells you what to prepare for; it does not estimate how well that Pokémon converts, so read threat level from playoff and top-cut conversion, and read frequency from usage.

Context engineering is the art and science of curating what information goes into an LLM's limited attention window from a constantly evolving universe of possible data. As prompt engineering evolves into context engineering, the focus shifts from finding the right prompt words to optimizing the entire context state, including system instructions, tools, MCP, external data, and message history. LLMs have a limited attention capacity, and studies on needle-in-a-haystack benchmarks reveal context rot: as token count increases, model accuracy at recalling specific information decreases. Every new token depletes the attention budget, and models develop n² pairwise relationships across n tokens, stretching architectural limits. Good context engineering finds the smallest possible set of high-signal tokens that maximize desired outcomes rather than adding more information. Organize prompts into distinct sections using XML tags or Markdown headers to delineate background information, instructions, tool guidance, and output descriptions. Unlike discrete prompt writing, context engineering is iterative—the curation phase happens each time we decide what to pass to the model in a loop, with agents cyclically refining generated data. A useful retrieval ladder follows llms.txt to topic summaries to focused Markdown notes to a bounded compiled view, with each layer remaining useful without agent-specific JavaScript or tool servers so content stays accessible when agent-specific enhancements are unavailable.

Build an LLM-friendly site in layers: publish canonical semantic pages with stable URLs and provenance; add llms.txt, topic summaries, and a bounded compiled Markdown view; provide narrow retrieval with filtering and truncation; add MCP only for workflows that benefit from a standard external integration; experiment with WebMCP only as progressive enhancement; and add consequential actions after authorization, confirmation, logging, and task-level evaluation exist. The governing rule is to optimize first for reliable reading, then for bounded action, so the content remains useful when every agent-specific enhancement is unavailable.

The llms.txt file is a lightweight, plain-text index that provides a discovery surface for LLMs reading a website. Unlike HTML sitemaps organized for humans, llms.txt lists documentation pages as a flat bullet list with titles and URLs, letting agents efficiently discover available documentation without parsing navigation menus or searching. The MCP documentation demonstrates this pattern with a root llms.txt at https://modelcontextprotocol.io/llms.txt that agents fetch first to understand what's available, then selectively retrieve specific pages. This creates a retrieval ladder: llms.txt → topic summaries → focused Markdown notes → compiled view. The benefit is reduced token consumption and faster discovery compared to browsing HTML pages designed for human presentation.

MCP follows a client-server architecture where an MCP host (AI application) creates one MCP client per MCP server, each maintaining a dedicated connection. The protocol consists of two layers: a JSON-RPC 2.0 data layer defining primitives (tools, resources, prompts, notifications) and a transport layer supporting stdio (local) and Streamable HTTP (remote) with OAuth authentication. Local servers use stdio for zero-overhead process communication; remote servers use HTTP with SSE streaming. The data layer primitives — tools for actions, resources for context data, prompts for templates — are transport-agnostic, allowing the same JSON-RPC messages over any transport. Servers declare capabilities via discovery; clients access features through structured RPC calls. This design keeps the protocol focused on context exchange without dictating how LLMs use the provided context.
