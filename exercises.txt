	- How does one invent the notion of a sigma-algebra? (Hint: Need to eventually obtain the Borel $\sigma$-algebra, which is needed for uniqueness when containing a topology. A $\sigma$-algebra serves as the parameter space for a measure; visualize it as sets or "blobs" where the complement of a blob is in the $\sigma$-algebra, the empty set is in the $\sigma$-algebra, and arbitrary countable unions of blobs are in the $\sigma$-algebra. A measure is a map assigning a non-negative extended real number to each set in a $\sigma$-algebra such that the empty set has measure zero and countable unions of disjoint sets satisfy countable additivity $\mu(\bigcup E_i) = \sum \mu(E_i)$. Disjointness prevents overlap. The key mental image is blobs, complements of blobs, and countable unions.)
	- How does one invent measurable functions? (Hint: A function is measurable if one can take preimages at the level of $\sigma$-algebras for maps between measurable/topological spaces, analogous to continuous functions in topology where preimages of open sets are open.)
	- How does one invent the integral with respect to a measure? (Hint: Note that the existence of the integral is a theorem summarized by the monotone convergence theorem. Given a measure space, assign non-negative measurable functions to integrals that are linear and satisfy the monotone convergence theorem, meaning if a monotone increasing sequence of functions converges pointwise, then their integrals converge to the integral of the limit. Lastly, integrating against an indicator function $\mathbf{1}_A$ recovers the measure $\mu(A)$. The key intuition is linear, non-interacting decomposition into a basis similar to Fourier analysis.)
	- How does one invent simple functions? (Hint: Define them as finite linear combinations of indicator functions taking on finitely many values, and approximate measurable functions using monotone convergence. The key image is finiteness and step approximations.)
	- How does one invent the notion of "almost everywhere"? (Hint: Measurable functions are considered equal if they agree up to a set of measure zero. The key image is gluing together boundaries of measure zero.)
	- How does one invent the notion of Fatou's lemma? (Hint: If a sequence of non-negative functions is not monotone, one gets an inequality for the limit inferior: the integral of the limit inferior is less than or equal to the limit inferior of the integrals, i.e., $\int \liminf f_n \, d\mu \le \liminf \int f_n \, d\mu$. This is proven by applying the monotone convergence theorem to the sequence of infimums. The key image is visualizing expectation and the lower bound of the integral of a shifting wave or sine curve.)
	- How does one invent integrable functions? (Hint: Define the space $L^1(E)$ of functions where $\int |f| \, d\mu < \infty$. Extend the integral to general functions by taking the difference $\int f^+ \, d\mu - \int f^- \, d\mu$, using Carathéodory's extension theorem where applicable.)
	- How does one invent and prove the dominated convergence theorem? (Hint: Let $f_n$ be a sequence in a measure space such that $f_n \to f$ almost everywhere. If there exists an integrable dominating function $g$ such that $|f_n| \le g$ almost everywhere, then $\lim \int f_n \, d\mu = \int f \, d\mu$. Prove this by applying Fatou's lemma to $g - f_n$ and $g + f_n$. The easiest intuition is a bounded envelope; domination acts as a compactness condition analogous to sequential weak compactness in $L^p$ spaces.)
	- How does one invent product sigma-algebras? (Hint: Take Cartesian products analogously to product topologies, generating the product $\sigma$-algebra from cylinder sets, and show the existence of a unique product measure that decomposes into the composition of marginal measures.)
	- How does one invent Fubini's theorem? (Hint: Map $(x_1, x_2)$ to iterated integrals across the tensor product of two $\sigma$-algebras, showing that under integrability conditions one can interchange the order of integration for $f(x_1, x_2) \in \mathcal{L}^1(E_1 \times E_2)$.)
	- How does one first encounter conditional probability theory? (Hint: Note the notation change and scale the probability measure first: $P(A \mid B) = P(A \cap B) / P(B)$, assuming event $B$ has occurred by taking the intersection. Next, allow $B$ to vary across disjoint events in a partition, define linear combinations integrating against the partition elements, and think of the probability space as being partitioned into compartments. Testing against random variable $X$ across these countably many disjoint events yields the discrete conditional expectation.)
	- How does one find the characterizing property of conditional expectation? (Hint: Given a sub-$\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{G}$, the conditional expectation $Y = \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$ is uniquely characterized by being $\mathcal{G}$-measurable, integrable, and satisfying $\mathbb{E}[Y \mathbf{1}_G] = \mathbb{E}[X \mathbf{1}_G]$ for all $G \in \mathcal{G}$. Prove this by computing expectations and using monotone convergence to swap expectation and infinite sums.)
	- How does one prove the existence and uniqueness of conditional expectation? (Hint: Prove uniqueness up to measure zero via functional analysis on Hilbert spaces. For $X \in L^2$, define $Y$ as the orthogonal projection of $X$ onto the closed subspace $L^2(\mathcal{G})$, using $X - Y \perp L^2(\mathcal{G})$. Extend to non-negative $X \in \mathcal{m}\mathcal{E}_+$ using monotone convergence of $X \wedge n$ and indicator tests. Finally, for arbitrary $X \in L^1$, decompose $X = X^+ - X^-$ and use linearity.)
	- What key properties does one want to prove about conditional expectation? (Hint: Total expectation $\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]] = \mathbb{E}[X]$, positivity $X \ge 0 \implies \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] \ge 0$, independence $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] = \mathbb{E}[X]$ if $X$ is independent of $\mathcal{G}$, linearity, monotone convergence, Fatou's lemma, dominated convergence theorem, Jensen's inequality, and the tower property $\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}_2] \mid \mathcal{G}_1] = \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}_1]$ for $\mathcal{G}_1 \subseteq \mathcal{G}_2$.)
	- How does one prove that for $X \in L^1$, the family of random variables $Y_\mathcal{G} = \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$ across all sub-$\sigma$-algebras $\mathcal{G} \subseteq \mathcal{F}$ is uniformly integrable? (Hint: Fix $\varepsilon > 0$. Apply Jensen's inequality to obtain $\mathbb{E}[|Y|] \le \mathbb{E}[|X|]$, then use Markov's inequality and the $\mathcal{G}$-measurability of $\mathbf{1}_{\{|Y| > \lambda\}}$ to bound $\mathbb{E}[|Y| \mathbf{1}_{\{|Y| > \lambda\}}] = \mathbb{E}[|X| \mathbf{1}_{\{|Y| > \lambda\}}] < \varepsilon$ uniformly for sufficiently large $\lambda$.)
	- How does one invent filtrations? (Hint: Define an increasing family of $\sigma$-algebras $(\mathcal{F}_n)_{n \ge 0}$ representing information accumulated up to time $n$.)
	- How does one invent stochastic processes? (Hint: Equip a filtered measure space with a collection or sequence of random variables $(X_n)$, and consider the information generated by their trajectories.)
	- How does one invent natural filtrations? (Hint: Define the minimal filtration $\mathcal{F}_n^X = \sigma(X_0, X_1, \dots, X_n)$ generated by the sequence of random variables up to time $n$.)
	- How does one invent adapted processes? (Hint: Require each random variable $X_n$ to be measurable with respect to $\mathcal{F}_n$, so that its value is fully determined by the information available at time $n$.)
	- How does one invent integrable processes? (Hint: Require that $\mathbb{E}[|X_n|] < \infty$ ($X_n \in L^1$) for all $n \ge 0$.)
	- How does one invent martingales? (Hint: Model a fair game where the best prediction of future values given all current and past information is the present value, i.e., $\mathbb{E}[X_{n+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_n] = X_n$.)
	- How does one invent the notion of a stopping time? (Hint: Define a random variable $T$ taking values in $\mathbb{N} \cup \{\infty\}$ such that the event $\{T \le n\} \in \mathcal{F}_n$ for all $n$, meaning whether the event has occurred can be decided using only information up to time $n$.)
	- How does one invent the optional stopping theorem? (Hint: Formulate conditions—such as bounded stopping times, bounded increments, or uniform integrability—under which the expected value of a stopped martingale equals its initial expectation, $\mathbb{E}[X_T] = \mathbb{E}[X_0]$.)

	- How does one invent the genus of a surface? (Hint: Holes and handles are preserved under homeomorphism and homotopy; algebraic topology assigns abelian groups to detect these holes algebraically.)
	- How does one invent the notion of a chain complex? (Hint: Define a sequence of abelian groups and boundary homomorphisms $(C_n, \partial_n)$ satisfying the algebraic boundary condition $\partial_n \circ \partial_{n+1} = 0$.)
	- How does one invent the notion of a cochain complex? (Hint: Dualize chain complexes by applying $\operatorname{Hom}(-, G)$ with coboundary maps $d^n = \partial_{n+1}^*$ satisfying $d^{n+1} \circ d^n = 0$.)
	- How does one invent homology? (Hint: Since $\partial^2 = 0$, the image of $\partial_{n+1}$ is contained in the kernel of $\partial_n$. Define the homology group as the quotient $H_n = \ker(\partial_n) / \operatorname{im}(\partial_{n+1})$ to measure non-bounding cycles.)
	- How does one invent the notion of a homology class? (Hint: Represent homology groups as equivalence classes of cycles modulo boundaries $[z] \in \ker \partial / \operatorname{im} \partial$.)
	- How does one invent the notion of cycles? (Hint: Cycles are elements in the kernel of $\partial_n$, algebraically capturing closed paths or loops with no boundary.)

	- How does one invent chain maps? (Hint: Define collections of homomorphisms $f_\# : C_n \to C_n'$ commuting with boundary operators $f_\# \circ \partial = \partial' \circ f_\#$, acting as morphisms in the category of chain complexes.)
	- Why is singular homology both advantageous and disadvantageous? (Hint: It is functorial and does not depend on a choice of triangulation, but singular chain groups are uncountably infinite and impossible to compute directly from the definition without structural tools like Mayer-Vietoris.)

	- What does it mean for a PDE to be well-posed in the sense of Hadamard? (Hint: A solution exists, is unique, and depends continuously on initial/boundary data.)
	- What is the transport equation? (Hint: $\partial_t u + b \cdot \nabla u = 0$, describing the advection of a density along characteristics.)
	- What are Laplace's, heat, and wave equations? (Hint: Prototypical elliptic ($\Delta u = 0$), parabolic ($\partial_t u - \Delta u = 0$), and hyperbolic ($\partial_{tt} u - \Delta u = 0$) PDEs.)
	- How are second-order linear PDEs classified? (Hint: By the eigenvalues of the principal coefficient matrix: all same sign (elliptic), one zero (parabolic), one opposite sign (hyperbolic).)
	- What does the Picard-Lindelöf theorem state? (Hint: Guarantees local existence and uniqueness of solutions to ODE initial value problems with Lipschitz continuous vector fields via Banach fixed-point theorem.)
	- What is the Cauchy-Kovalevskaya theorem? (Hint: Guarantees the local existence and uniqueness of real analytic solutions to non-characteristic analytic Cauchy problems.)
	- What is a non-characteristic hypersurface? (Hint: A surface $\Phi(x) = 0$ where the principal symbol of the differential operator does not vanish on the normal vector $\nabla \Phi$.)
	- What is Hölder space $C^{k, \gamma}(\Omega)$? (Hint: The Banach space of $k$-times continuously differentiable functions whose $k$-th partial derivatives are Hölder continuous with exponent $\gamma \in (0, 1]$.)
	- What is $L^p(\Omega)$ space? (Hint: The Banach space of equivalence classes of measurable functions satisfying $\|u\|_{L^p} = \left(\int_\Omega |u|^p \, dx\right)^{1/p} < \infty$.)
	- What is a weak (distributional) derivative? (Hint: A locally integrable function $v = D^\alpha u$ satisfying $\int_\Omega u D^\alpha \phi \, dx = (-1)^{|\alpha|} \int_\Omega v \phi \, dx$ for all test functions $\phi \in C_c^\infty(\Omega)$.)
	- What is a Sobolev space $W^{k, p}(\Omega)$? (Hint: The Banach space of functions in $L^p(\Omega)$ whose weak derivatives up to order $k$ exist and belong to $L^p(\Omega)$; denoted $H^k(\Omega)$ for $p=2$.)
	- What are standard mollifiers? (Hint: Smooth, compactly supported bump functions $\eta_\varepsilon(x) = \varepsilon^{-n} \eta(x/\varepsilon)$ used to approximate $L^p$ and Sobolev functions by smooth functions via convolution $u_\varepsilon = u * \eta_\varepsilon$.)
	- What are Sobolev inequalities? (Hint: Embedding theorems (Gagliardo-Nirenberg-Sobolev, Morrey) that trade weak differentiability for higher integrability or Hölder continuity.)
	- What is uniform ellipticity? (Hint: There exists $\theta > 0$ such that $\sum_{i, j} a_{ij}(x) \xi_i \xi_j \ge \theta |\xi|^2$ for all $\xi \in \mathbb{R}^n$.)
	- How does one formulate weak solutions for elliptic boundary value problems? (Hint: Multiply by test functions, integrate by parts to reduce derivative orders, and apply the Lax-Milgram theorem in $H_0^1(\Omega)$.)
